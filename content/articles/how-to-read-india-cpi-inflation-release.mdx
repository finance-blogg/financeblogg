Once a month, headlines say inflation has “eased” or “surged”. The source document is usually a **Consumer Price Index (CPI)** press note from the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation (**MoSPI**). CPI is a price **index** for a basket of goods and services. **Inflation** is how fast that index has risen over a year (or another period).

This explainer is about reading that note. It will not forecast next month, and it will not tell you whether onions in your mandi match the national average.

<KeyTakeaways>
- India’s monetary-policy inflation target is set in terms of **CPI**, not the Sensex and not wholesale prices.
- **Headline** CPI is the all-groups number. **Food** and **fuel** can swing it even when the rest of the basket is quiet.
- The print is a **national, weighted average**. Your city and your grocery bag can differ.
- One month does not reset your [home-loan EMI](/blog/how-loan-emi-is-calculated). Policy uses a run of data; see [how to read an RBI statement](/blog/how-to-read-an-rbi-monetary-policy-statement).
- For household planning, inflation is why idle cash loses [purchasing power](/blog/how-inflation-affects-your-savings).
</KeyTakeaways>

## What the CPI basket is

MoSPI constructs CPI from prices collected across rural and urban centres, weighted by how households in the survey spent their money in the base period. Combined CPI (rural + urban) is the series most news and the MPC talk about.

The basket includes food and beverages, pan/tobacco, clothing, housing, fuel and light, and miscellaneous (health, transport, education, personal care, and so on). Weights are not equal. Food still carries a large weight in India’s CPI, which is why vegetable spikes show up in the headline.

A **base year** is the year the index is set near 100. Later indices are relative to that. When the base year is revised, levels jump; year-on-year inflation is still the fair comparison if MoSPI publishes it on the new series.

<Callout type="verify">
The current base year, group weights and publication calendar are on [mospi.gov.in](https://www.mospi.gov.in). If a chart on social media has no source line and no “combined CPI, year-on-year”, ignore it.
</Callout>

## The numbers on the first page

Open the press note, not a TV ticker.

**Index level** for the month (combined, rural, urban).

**Inflation rate** — usually **year-on-year**: this month versus the same month last year. That is the headline.

**Month-on-month** change — useful for whether prices rose *this* month, but noisier.

**Group-wise inflation** — food and beverages, fuel and light, housing, miscellaneous.

**CFPI** (consumer food price index) if published — food’s own story.

Analysts often talk about **CPI excluding food and fuel** (sometimes called “core” in commentary). That is a derived cut. If you use it, say so. It is not a substitute for the headline target.

<Callout type="example" title="Headline 4%, food 8%">
Illustrative only. Combined CPI inflation prints at 4% year-on-year, while the food group prints at 8% and fuel is negative.

Households who spend a larger share on food than the CPI weight will *feel* poorer than 4%. Households whose EMI is linked to a policy rate may *feel* little this month. Both can be looking at the same official note.
</Callout>

## What the print does not decide that afternoon

**Your grocery bill.** Local mandi prices, brand discounts and what you actually buy diverge from the national basket.

**Your FD rate.** Banks react to policy, liquidity and their own funding, with a lag.

**Your EMI.** Floating-rate loans move when the lender resets the benchmark, not when MoSPI publishes a PDF. A single CPI print is one input to the [MPC](/blog/how-to-read-an-rbi-monetary-policy-statement).

**Income tax slabs.** Those are in the Budget and the tax law, not in the CPI note.

## How to read a “surprise”

Ask four questions:

1. Is this **year-on-year** combined CPI, or a group, or wholesale (WPI)?
2. Did **food** or **fuel** do the work?
3. Is the move a **base effect** (last year was unusually high or low)?
4. Does the **three- or six-month** trend agree, or is this one spike?

Base effect is the most common trick of interpretation: inflation can fall because last year’s price jump has rolled out of the comparison, even if prices did not fall this month.

## Using CPI without becoming a macro pundit

For a household:

- If food inflation is persistent, the [budget](/blog/how-to-create-a-monthly-budget-in-india) needs a higher grocery line, not a debate on core.
- If headline inflation stays high, cash and low-rate deposits lose purchasing power faster — that is an [allocation](/blog/what-asset-allocation-means-for-beginners) issue for long-term money, not a reason to gamble the rent.
- If you are reading the note to guess the next repo move, you are speculating. The MPC statement is the decision; CPI is one of the inputs.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think the useful civic habit is to open the MoSPI PDF once a month, write down headline and food inflation, and ignore adjectives. “Softening” and “hot” are comments. The table is the news.
</Callout>

## A two-minute ritual

1. Open the latest CPI press note on MoSPI.
2. Note combined year-on-year inflation, food, and fuel.
3. Compare with last month’s note you saved — not with memory.
4. If a WhatsApp forward disagrees with the PDF, keep the PDF.

Wholesale Price Index releases are a different survey (producers, not household consumers). Do not mix WPI and CPI in one sentence unless you know why both appeared.
