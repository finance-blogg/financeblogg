Many banks offer a **sweep-in** or **auto-sweep** facility linked to a savings (or current) account. In plain language: when your balance crosses a threshold the bank sets, excess money may be moved into a deposit-like product to earn a different interest rate; when you need money, units or portions may sweep back.

Product names and rules differ by bank. This article explains the idea so you can read your bank’s terms with clearer questions—not so you pick a specific bank product.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Sweep-in links a **transaction account** with an automatic move into a **deposit** when balances are high enough.
- Liquidity is usually better than a long locked [fixed deposit](/blog/savings-account-vs-fixed-deposit), but terms, minimums and interest calculation are **bank-specific**.
- Sweeps are not a substitute for a planned [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save) you understand.
- Always read the bank’s product disclosure for thresholds, tenure break-up and premature sweep-out rules.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Why banks offer it

Savers often leave large idle balances in savings accounts. A sweep tries to put excess cash into a higher-interest deposit structure while still letting everyday debits (UPI, ECS, cheques) hit the savings side first.

Whether the effective interest is attractive depends on the bank’s rates, how often money sweeps, and how interest is calculated when money returns.

## Typical moving parts

Ask your bank to explain, in writing if possible:

1. **Threshold** — above what balance does a sweep trigger?
2. **Chunk size** — does money move in fixed multiples (for example, ₹5,000 units)?
3. **Where it goes** — a fixed deposit, a flexi deposit, or another labelled product?
4. **Sweep-out order** — which deposit breaks first when you spend?
5. **Interest** — savings rate vs deposit rate, and on what balance.
6. **Penalties** — any cost if a deposit is broken early by a sweep-out.

<Callout type="example" title="Illustrative only">
Suppose a bank sweeps every ₹10,000 above ₹50,000 into short deposits. If your balance is ₹72,000, ₹20,000 might sit in swept deposits and ₹52,000 in savings—exact maths depend entirely on that bank’s rules. This is not a real offer.
</Callout>

## Sweep-in vs keeping a separate FD

| Point | Sweep-in | Separate FD you book yourself |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Effort | Automatic once enabled | You choose amount and tenure |
| Clarity | Can be harder to see on statements | Usually a clear FD receipt |
| Goal fit | Good for idle cash buffer | Better when you knowingly lock money |

If you already use [recurring deposits](/blog/how-recurring-deposits-work) or deliberate FDs for goals, a sweep is optional, not mandatory.

## Points people miss

- Swept amounts may still count toward the same bank for [DICGC deposit insurance](/blog/what-deposit-insurance-covers-in-india) ceilings—do not assume unlimited cover.
- Statement lines can look confusing; learn the bank’s narration codes.
- A sweep does not fix overspending; pair it with a [budget](/blog/how-to-create-a-monthly-budget-in-india).
- Tax on interest still applies as per Income Tax rules for the interest you earn—confirm with a tax professional for your case.

<Callout type="note">
Enable a sweep only after you understand the threshold and break rules. If the bank cannot explain them simply, pause.
</Callout>
