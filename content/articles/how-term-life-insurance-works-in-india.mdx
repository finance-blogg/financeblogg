Term life insurance is a contract that pays a lump sum to your nominee if you die while the policy is in force, provided the claim is payable under the terms. There is usually **no maturity payout** if you outlive the term. That is the feature, not a defect: you are paying for a defined risk over a defined period, not for a savings account wrapped as insurance.

This article explains that contract. It does not recommend an insurer, a rider, or a rupee amount of cover.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A term plan pays the **sum assured** on a covered death during the term. If you survive the term, cover ends unless you renew or buy a new policy, if then eligible.
- The premium is for **protection**. Mixing it with investment products changes the cost and the purpose.
- **Disclosure** on the proposal form (health, income, existing policies, habits) is what later claims are tested against.
- A **nominee** receives the claim proceeds; nomination does not by itself settle a legal dispute about who owns the money.
- Exclusions, waiting periods and a suicide clause are in the policy wording, not in the advertisement.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Term cover vs other life policies

**Term insurance:** high cover for a relatively low premium, no (or no meaningful) payout if you live.

**Endowment, money-back, ULIPs:** combine insurance with savings or market-linked investment. The premium per rupee of life cover is typically much higher. Those products can be valid for people who want that mix; they are a poor way to buy a large death benefit cheaply.

If your problem is “my family would not manage the home loan and living costs if I died this year”, start by understanding term cover. If your problem is “I want a forced savings plan”, that is a different problem, closer to [financial goals](/blog/how-to-set-financial-goals-in-india) than to a death benefit.

Health insurance pays hospital bills. Term insurance does not. Compare health policies on [waiting periods and sub-limits](/blog/how-to-compare-health-insurance-policies), not on the life-cover brochure.

## Sum assured and tenure

**Sum assured** is the lump sum the insurer pays on a valid death claim (or the instalment structure you chose, if the plan pays monthly income). There is no official formula. People often start from:

- outstanding loans that should not pass to the family;
- years of living costs for dependants, minus spouse income and existing assets;
- known dated costs such as children’s education.

Those are conversation starters, not rules. Too little cover leaves a hole. Far too much cover can mean a proposal is declined or loaded, because insurers underwrite income and insurable interest.

**Tenure** should at least cover the years someone depends on your income, or the remaining years of a large loan. A 30-year-old with a 20-year home loan and young children is looking at a different term than a 50-year-old whose children are independent. Buying a very short term to cheapen the premium can leave a gap in the years you still have debt.

Premiums rise with age, tobacco use, health, occupation and cover. A medical exam is common above insurer-specific limits.

<Callout type="example" title="Cover is not a lifestyle multiple from a reel">
Illustrative thinking, not a recommendation.

A family has a ₹40 lakh home-loan outstanding, one earning spouse, and two children who will need support for 15 years. The useful question is: if the earning spouse dies next year, which rupee amounts must arrive, and for how long? A slogan such as “15 times salary” might overshoot or undershoot that household. Do the cash-flow list first.
</Callout>

## What the proposal form is doing

Underwriting is the insurer deciding whether to accept you, at what premium, with what exclusions. Lies by omission are a common reason claims are disputed.

Disclose:

- existing life policies and pending proposals;
- income and occupation;
- medical history, medication, hospitalisations;
- tobacco and alcohol, as asked;
- hazardous hobbies or foreign residence, as asked.

If a medical test contradicts the form, the form still matters. If your health changes after the policy is issued, rules depend on the contract and on whether you are still in a contestability period. Do not guess; read the policy and ask the insurer in writing.

## Exclusions, riders and the suicide clause

Policies list situations that are **not** covered, or are covered only after a waiting period. A suicide clause is standard in life insurance: if death by suicide occurs within a specified period from inception (commonly 12 months, confirm in **your** policy), the contract may limit the payout to premiums paid, not the sum assured.

**Riders** (accidental death, disability, critical illness) are optional add-ons with their own definitions. A critical-illness rider is not a substitute for health insurance. An accidental-death rider pays extra only for deaths that meet its definition of accident.

<Callout type="verify">
Free-look periods, grace periods for unpaid premiums, revival rules and suicide-clause timelines are set in regulations and in your policy document. Read the Customer Information Sheet and the policy wording; do not rely on a sales illustration.
</Callout>

## Nominees, assignments and loans

Name a nominee and keep the name, relationship and contact details updated after marriage, children or a death in the family. For a married person, understanding how nomination interacts with the legal heirs is worth a careful read of the policy and, if the amount is large, legal advice. Nomination is meant to give the insurer a clear person to pay; it is not always the last word in a family dispute.

If the policy is **assigned** to a lender (common with a home loan’s group cover or a pledged policy), the lender may have first claim. Group cover from an employer or a bank often ends when the job or the loan ends. Do not count it as permanent personal cover.

## Premiums, GST and tax

Term premiums are paid monthly, yearly, or as a limited-pay schedule. GST applies to life insurance premiums as per the then-current rate. Tax treatment of premiums and of the death benefit depends on the law in force for that year and on how the policy is structured.

<Callout type="verify">
Deductions for life insurance premiums depend on the tax regime you choose and on current limits. Confirm on the [Income Tax Department](https://www.incometax.gov.in) site. Do not buy a policy only for a deduction; the deduction is usually small next to the decision about cover.
</Callout>

## If a claim is needed

The nominee (or claimant) typically:

1. informs the insurer as soon as possible;
2. submits the death certificate, policy documents, claimant identity, and forms the insurer lists;
3. cooperates with any investigation the insurer is allowed to make;
4. keeps copies of everything.

If the claim is delayed or rejected and you disagree, use the insurer’s grievance officer, then IRDAI’s [Bima Bharosa](https://bimabharosa.irdai.gov.in) portal, then the [Insurance Ombudsman](https://www.cioins.co.in) where applicable.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think term insurance is easier to understand when you treat it like a fire extinguisher: you hope not to use it, you check it still exists after you change jobs or loans, and you do not buy a complicated savings wrapper because the extinguisher felt “wasteful” in a year nobody died. Wasteful is the wrong word for a risk you cannot absorb.
</Callout>

Buy only from a registered life insurer, read the illustration and the wording, and keep the policy document where the nominee can find it. That last step is unglamorous and more useful than a new rider you do not understand.
