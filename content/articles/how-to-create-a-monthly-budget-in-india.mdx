A budget is simply a plan for your money before the month begins, rather than an explanation of where it went after the month ends. You do not need an app, a spreadsheet template or an accounting background. You need an honest list of what comes in, what must go out, and what you want to set aside.

This guide walks through a method that works for salaried and self-employed readers in India, including the expenses that generic advice often forgets: EMIs, money sent to parents, school fees paid once a term, and the festival season.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Start from your **actual take-home income**, not your CTC.
- Track one month of real spending before you set limits.
- Convert **annual and irregular costs** into monthly amounts so they stop surprising you.
- Move savings out of your account **on salary day**, before you start spending.
- Rules like 50/30/20 are starting points. Your rent, EMIs and family responsibilities may need a different split.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Step 1: Work out your real monthly income

Use the money that actually reaches your bank account, not your cost-to-company (CTC) or gross salary. Your payslip usually shows deductions such as provident fund contributions, professional tax and tax deducted at source (TDS). What remains after those deductions is what you can budget.

If your income varies, as it does for freelancers, business owners, commission-based roles and gig workers, look at the money you received over the last six to twelve months and budget using a figure towards the **lower end** of that range. In a good month, the extra goes to savings or a buffer. In a lean month, your essentials are still covered.

Include other regular inflows only if they are reliable, such as rent you receive. Leave out one-off items like a bonus or a tax refund; plan for those separately when they arrive.

## Step 2: List your fixed monthly expenses

Fixed expenses are roughly the same every month and are hard to cut quickly. Typical examples:

- rent or home loan EMI, and maintenance charges;
- other loan EMIs, such as a car, two-wheeler, education or personal loan;
- school or college fees, converted to a monthly figure;
- regular support you send to parents or other family members;
- mobile, internet, electricity and cooking gas (use an average if they vary);
- domestic help, if you employ someone;
- subscriptions you intend to keep.

Write down the actual amounts. If you are not sure, your bank statement and UPI app history will show them.

## Step 3: Track one month of variable spending

Variable expenses change month to month: groceries, fuel and commuting, eating out, food delivery, shopping, medical bills, entertainment and travel. This is where most budgets go wrong, because people estimate these from memory and underestimate them.

Before you set any limits, track a full month of spending. In India this is easier than it sounds, because so much spending is digital:

- **UPI apps** show a full history of payments.
- **Bank and credit card statements** list every debit. Our guide on [how to read a bank statement](/blog/how-to-read-a-bank-statement) explains the codes.
- **Cash** is the hard part. Note cash withdrawals and, if possible, jot down what cash was spent on.

Group what you find into a handful of categories. Ten or twelve is plenty; more than that becomes tedious to maintain.

## Step 4: Plan for annual and irregular expenses

Many Indian households are thrown off by large expenses that are predictable but do not arrive monthly:

- festivals such as Diwali, Eid, Christmas, Onam or Durga Puja, including gifts and travel;
- weddings in the extended family;
- school admission fees, books and uniforms at the start of the academic year;
- annual health, vehicle or life insurance premiums;
- property tax, vehicle servicing and home repairs.

The fix is a **sinking fund**: estimate the yearly cost of each item, divide by twelve, and set that amount aside every month. When the expense arrives, the money is already there and your monthly budget is not disturbed.

<Callout type="example">
If you expect to spend about ₹24,000 across the festival season and ₹12,000 on an annual vehicle insurance premium, that is ₹36,000 a year, or ₹3,000 a month. Moving ₹3,000 into a separate savings account or recurring deposit each month covers both.
</Callout>

## Step 5: Choose a budgeting method

There is no single correct method. Pick one you will actually follow.

| Method | How it works | Suits people who |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 50/30/20 | Roughly 50% of take-home pay for needs, 30% for wants, 20% for savings and extra debt repayment | Want a simple starting framework |
| Zero-based | Every rupee is assigned a job (spending, saving or debt) until income minus allocations equals zero | Like detail and want tight control |
| Separate accounts or "envelopes" | Money is split into different accounts or wallets for bills, spending and savings | Overspend when everything sits in one account |

The 50/30/20 split is a widely used rule of thumb, not a regulation or a target that suits everyone. In large cities, rent alone can take a big share of income, and many people support parents or siblings. If your needs come to 60% of take-home pay, the answer is not to feel guilty but to decide consciously where the remaining 40% goes.

## Step 6: Pay yourself first

The most reliable way to save is to move money out of your main account **as soon as your salary arrives**, rather than saving whatever is left at the end of the month.

You can set up standing instructions with your bank, a recurring deposit, or a systematic investment plan (SIP) dated a day or two after your salary credit. Then budget your spending from what remains. If you do not yet have an emergency fund, build that first; our guide on [emergency funds](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save) explains how much to aim for and where to keep it.

## A worked example

<Callout type="example" title="A ₹60,000 monthly take-home budget">
The figures below are invented to show the method. They are not a recommendation for how anyone should spend.

**Needs: ₹35,000 (about 58%)**

- Rent: ₹15,000
- Groceries and household supplies: ₹8,000
- Electricity, internet, mobile and gas: ₹3,500
- Commute: ₹3,000
- Health insurance premium (annual premium divided by 12): ₹1,500
- Support to parents: ₹4,000

**Wants: ₹9,000 (about 15%)**

- Eating out and food delivery: ₹3,000
- Subscriptions: ₹700
- Shopping: ₹3,000
- Entertainment and short trips: ₹2,300

**Savings and sinking funds: ₹16,000 (about 27%)**

- Emergency fund: ₹5,000
- Long-term investing: ₹6,000
- Festivals and gifts: ₹3,000
- Annual vehicle insurance and repairs: ₹2,000
</Callout>

Notice that this household's "needs" are well above 50%, mainly because of rent and family support, but it spends less on wants and still saves more than 20%. That is a reasonable outcome. The value of the exercise is that every rupee has a purpose, and the trade-offs are visible.

## Review the budget every month

A budget is a draft, not a contract. At the end of each month, spend fifteen minutes comparing what you planned with what happened:

1. Which categories went over, and why?
2. Was the overspend a one-off, or does the limit need to change?
3. Did your savings transfers go through on time?
4. Is there a large expense coming in the next three months that needs a sinking fund?

Expect the first two or three months to be messy. Most people need a few cycles before their estimates become accurate.

## Common budgeting mistakes

- **Budgeting from CTC or gross salary**, which overstates the money you actually have.
- **Forgetting annual expenses**, then treating them as "emergencies" each time.
- **Setting unrealistic limits**, such as cutting food delivery to zero overnight, and abandoning the budget after two weeks.
- **Tracking too many categories**, which makes the budget hard to keep up.
- **Ignoring small recurring payments**, such as several subscriptions renewing automatically.
- **Not involving your household.** If you share expenses with a partner or family, agree on the plan together.

## Limitations of any budget

A budget cannot fix an income that does not cover essential costs. If your fixed expenses and EMIs consistently leave nothing for savings, the problem may need bigger changes, such as reducing housing costs, restructuring debt, or increasing income, rather than tighter tracking. If you are struggling with debt repayments, contact your lender early to discuss options, and be careful of anyone who charges a fee to "settle" your loans.
