Indian bank deposits are insured, but not in the way many people assume. The insurance is not a guarantee that a bank cannot fail, and it is not a promise that every rupee you hold is protected. It is a legal cover, run by the Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC), with a rupee ceiling and clear exclusions.

This article explains that cover. It is not a ranking of banks.

<KeyTakeaways>
- DICGC currently insures eligible bank deposits up to **₹5 lakh per depositor per bank**, for principal and interest combined.
- Deposits in **different branches of the same bank** are added together for that limit.
- Cover is separate at **different banks**.
- The premium is paid by the **bank**, not deducted from your account.
- NBFCs, companies, and deposits that DICGC lists as exempt are **not** the same as an insured bank deposit.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Who provides the cover

DICGC is a wholly owned subsidiary of the Reserve Bank of India. Banks that are in the deposit insurance scheme pay a premium to DICGC. You do not file a separate insurance proposal, and you do not see a premium line on your [bank statement](/blog/how-to-read-a-bank-statement).

If a bank is placed under restrictions that stop withdrawals, or is liquidated, DICGC’s role is to settle the insured amount for eligible deposits, subject to the Act and the facts of that case.

## The ₹5 lakh limit

With effect from 4 February 2020, each depositor is insured up to **₹5,00,000** for principal **and** accrued interest held in the **same right and same capacity** at one bank. DICGC’s own example is useful: if you have ₹4,95,000 plus ₹4,000 interest, the combined ₹4,99,000 is within the limit. If the principal is already ₹5 lakh, extra interest sits above the ceiling.

<Callout type="verify">
The ₹5 lakh limit has been revised in the past and can be revised again with government approval. Check [DICGC’s FAQs](https://dicgc.org.in/FAQs) for the figure in force when you read this.
</Callout>

### Branches of the same bank are one bucket

If you keep ₹3 lakh in a savings account at one branch and ₹3 lakh in a [fixed deposit](/blog/savings-account-vs-fixed-deposit) at another branch of the **same** bank, DICGC treats that as ₹6 lakh with one depositor. Only ₹5 lakh is insured. Spreading money across branches of one bank does not multiply cover.

### Different banks are separate

The same person can have insured deposits at Bank A and Bank B. Each bank is counted on its own, again up to the ceiling, for deposits in the same right and same capacity.

That is not a suggestion to open accounts everywhere. It is how the rule is written. More banks also mean more KYC, more statements, and more ways to lose track of nominations.

## What "same right and same capacity" means

DICGC does not always add every account with your name on it into one pile. Capacity matters. A savings account in your individual name is not automatically the same bucket as money you hold as a guardian, or as a partner, or in a joint capacity that the rules treat as different.

Joint accounts are a frequent source of confusion. Whether two holders each get a separate limit depends on how the account is held, not on how you describe it in conversation. Read DICGC’s examples for joint accounts, and ask the bank how the account is recorded, before you assume a doubled ceiling.

<Callout type="example" title="Illustrative, not a claim on a real bank">
Priya has ₹4 lakh in a savings account and ₹2 lakh in an FD at the same bank, both in her sole name. For DICGC, that is ₹6 lakh in one capacity. ₹5 lakh is within the current ceiling; ₹1 lakh is not insured.

If Priya instead holds ₹4 lakh at Bank A and ₹2 lakh at Bank B, each bank’s cover is calculated separately, still subject to the rules for capacity and eligible deposits.
</Callout>

## What is typically insured

DICGC insures **bank deposits**: savings, current, fixed, recurring, and similar deposit accounts at insured commercial banks (including small finance banks, payments banks, regional rural banks and local area banks) and at cooperative banks that are in the scheme.

It does **not** exist to cover:

- deposits of governments, or inter-bank deposits, or deposits received outside India, or other exemptions listed by DICGC;
- land development banks and **NBFCs**, which are outside this mandated bank-deposit cover;
- investments that are not bank deposits, such as mutual fund units, bonds you hold in your demat, or company deposits.

A recurring deposit at an insured bank is a deposit. A [SIP in a mutual fund](/blog/understanding-sip-and-mutual-fund-investing) is not a bank deposit and is not a DICGC product.

## What insurance does not do

- It does not make a weak bank strong.
- It does not cover the full balance if you are above the ceiling.
- It does not replace the need for a nomination and updated KYC. Those affect who can claim, not whether the deposit was eligible.
- It does not apply because an app looks like a bank. Confirm you are dealing with a licensed bank, and that the product is a deposit with that bank.

<Callout type="warning" title="Company deposits and “high interest” schemes">
A higher advertised rate is sometimes a sign that you have left insured bank deposits and entered a credit product or a company deposit. Those can be legal, but they are not DICGC cover. If you cannot explain which licensed bank holds the money, pause.
</Callout>

## Practical checks

1. Add up **all** balances at one bank: savings, FDs, RDs, current accounts, in the same capacity.
2. Include interest that has accrued, not just the principal you remember.
3. Keep nominations and joint-holder details in writing.
4. If a large sum will sit for a while, decide whether you are comfortable with the uninsured slice at that bank.
5. For day-to-day money, the more immediate risks are still phishing, wrong UPI numbers and sharing OTPs, not bank failure.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think deposit insurance is a backstop, not an investment strategy. Use it to understand the ceiling. Do not treat “open an account at every bank” as a plan. Clarity about what is a bank deposit, and what is not, matters more than collecting passbooks.
</Callout>

If a bank is in the news, read the RBI or DICGC notice, not forwarded images. Our explainer on [reading an RBI policy statement](/blog/how-to-read-an-rbi-monetary-policy-statement) is about interest rates; for a specific bank, the relevant document is the official press release about **that** bank.
