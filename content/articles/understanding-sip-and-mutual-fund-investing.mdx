A **systematic investment plan (SIP)** is one of the most talked-about ideas in Indian personal finance. It is often presented as a product in itself, but it is really just a **method of investing**: putting a fixed amount into a mutual fund at regular intervals. To use it sensibly, you need to understand what a mutual fund is, what you are paying for, and what can go wrong.

This article is educational. It does not recommend any fund or category.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A mutual fund pools money from many investors and invests it according to a stated objective. You own **units**, and their value is the **NAV**.
- A SIP invests a fixed amount regularly. It brings discipline and averages your purchase cost, but it **does not guarantee profits or prevent losses**.
- **Direct plans** have lower costs than regular plans because no distributor commission is paid from the fund.
- Match the type of fund to **how long you can stay invested** and how much fall in value you can tolerate.
- Mutual fund gains are taxable, and the rules differ for equity and debt funds.
</KeyTakeaways>

## What a mutual fund is

A mutual fund collects money from many investors and invests it in a portfolio of shares, bonds, money market instruments or other assets, according to the scheme's stated objective. It is managed by an **asset management company (AMC)** and overseen by trustees. Mutual funds in India are regulated by the **Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI)**.

When you invest, you receive **units**. The price of a unit is the **net asset value (NAV)**, calculated as the value of the fund's assets minus its liabilities, divided by the number of units. For most schemes, NAV is calculated every business day. If the fund's investments rise in value, the NAV rises; if they fall, so does the NAV.

## Types of mutual funds

SEBI groups schemes into broad categories so that investors can compare like with like:

| Category | Mainly invests in | General characteristics |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Equity funds | Company shares | Higher potential for growth over long periods, with larger short-term ups and downs |
| Debt funds | Bonds, government securities, money market instruments | Usually less volatile than equity, but exposed to interest rate and credit risk |
| Hybrid funds | A mix of equity and debt | Risk and return depend on the mix |
| Solution-oriented funds | Built around goals such as retirement or children's education | Often come with a lock-in |
| Other funds | Including index funds, exchange-traded funds (ETFs) and fund of funds | Many are "passive", tracking an index rather than picking investments |

Within equity funds, there are further categories such as large-cap, mid-cap, small-cap, flexi-cap and sectoral funds, each with different risk levels. **Equity Linked Savings Schemes (ELSS)** are equity funds with a three-year lock-in.

**Active vs passive.** An actively managed fund has a fund manager choosing investments, aiming to do better than a benchmark. A passive fund, such as an index fund, simply tracks a benchmark index and usually charges less.

## What you pay: expense ratio, loads and plans

- **Expense ratio (TER).** The annual cost of running the fund, deducted from the fund's assets and reflected in the NAV. SEBI caps the maximum TER. A difference of even one percentage point a year adds up over long periods.
- **Exit load.** A charge some funds apply if you redeem within a specified period. It is disclosed in the scheme documents.
- **Direct vs regular plans.** Every scheme has a **direct plan**, bought straight from the AMC or through platforms that offer direct plans, and a **regular plan**, bought through a distributor who earns a commission from the fund. Regular plans have a higher expense ratio to pay that commission. Neither is wrong; with a regular plan you are paying for the distributor's service, so make sure you are getting it.

## How a SIP works

With a SIP, you instruct the fund to invest a fixed amount, such as ₹2,000 or ₹5,000, on a fixed date each month, usually through an automatic debit (NACH mandate) from your bank account. Each instalment buys units at that day's NAV.

### Rupee cost averaging

Because the amount is fixed, you buy **more units when the NAV is low** and **fewer units when it is high**. Over time, this averages your cost per unit.

<Callout type="example" title="Five monthly instalments of ₹5,000">
The NAVs below are invented to illustrate the mechanism. They are not a forecast.

| Month | NAV (₹) | Units bought |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | 50 | 100 |
| 2 | 40 | 125 |
| 3 | 25 | 200 |
| 4 | 40 | 125 |
| 5 | 50 | 100 |

- Total invested: ₹25,000
- Total units: 650
- Average cost per unit: ₹25,000 ÷ 650 ≈ ₹38.46
- Value at a NAV of ₹50: 650 × ₹50 = ₹32,500

The NAV ended where it started, but the investor gained because the SIP bought more units when prices were low. If the NAV had kept falling and not recovered, the investor would have had a loss.
</Callout>

Rupee cost averaging smooths the price you pay. It **does not** guarantee a profit, and in a market that rises steadily, investing a lump sum at the start would have done better. The bigger practical benefit of a SIP is often behavioural: it makes investing automatic and reduces the temptation to time the market.

### Time matters more than timing

<Callout type="example" title="Hypothetical illustration, not a projection">
Assume ₹5,000 a month invested at a **hypothetical** return of 8% a year, compounded monthly. Real returns vary from year to year, can be negative, and are not guaranteed.

- **After 10 years**: ₹6 lakh invested, illustrative value about ₹9.2 lakh.
- **After 20 years**: ₹12 lakh invested, illustrative value about ₹29.6 lakh.

Doubling the time more than triples the illustrative value, because returns themselves earn returns. The same effect works against you with costs: a higher expense ratio compounds too.
</Callout>

## The risks you take

Mutual fund investments are subject to market risks. The main ones are:

- **Market risk.** Equity fund NAVs can fall sharply, sometimes by a large percentage in a short period, and take years to recover.
- **Interest rate risk.** When interest rates rise, the prices of existing bonds fall, which can reduce debt fund NAVs, especially for longer-duration funds.
- **Credit risk.** A company or institution whose bonds the fund holds may fail to pay.
- **Liquidity risk.** In stressed markets, some investments can be hard to sell.
- **Concentration risk.** Sectoral and thematic funds depend heavily on one part of the economy.

Every scheme displays a **riskometer**, a SEBI-mandated label rating its risk on a scale from "Low" to "Very High". Check it and read the **Scheme Information Document (SID)** or Key Information Memorandum before you invest.

## How mutual fund gains are taxed

You pay tax when you **redeem** (sell) units at a gain, or on dividends (IDCW) when they are paid out. Broadly:

- **Equity-oriented funds**: gains on units held for more than 12 months are long-term and taxed at a concessional rate above an annual exemption limit; gains on units held for 12 months or less are short-term and taxed at a higher flat rate.
- **Debt funds**: for units bought on or after 1 April 2023, gains are generally taxed at your slab rate, regardless of how long you hold them.
- **Each SIP instalment counts as a separate purchase**, with its own holding period. When you redeem, units bought first are treated as sold first.
- **ELSS** investments can qualify for a deduction under the old tax regime only. Each SIP instalment in ELSS has its own three-year lock-in.

<Callout type="verify">
Capital gains rates and exemption limits were revised in July 2024 and can change in any Finance Act. India's Income-tax Act, 2025 also replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026. Check the current rates on the Income Tax Department website or with a tax professional before redeeming.
</Callout>

## Starting a SIP: practical steps

1. **Complete KYC.** You need PAN, address proof and identity verification. KYC done once is generally valid across fund houses.
2. **Decide the goal and horizon.** Money needed in the next few years usually does not belong in equity funds.
3. **Choose how to invest**: directly with the AMC, through a platform offering direct plans, through an AMFI-registered distributor (who should have an ARN), or with a SEBI-registered investment adviser who charges a fee for advice.
4. **Read the scheme documents**, including the riskometer, expense ratio and exit load.
5. **Set up the mandate** and choose a SIP date shortly after your salary credit.

Check that any adviser is registered with SEBI, and any distributor with AMFI. Be cautious of anyone promising guaranteed or fixed returns from mutual funds; that is not how they work.

## Common mistakes

- **Chasing last year's top performer.** Past performance does not reliably predict future performance.
- **Stopping SIPs during market falls.** This locks in the averaging benefit at the wrong time, if your goal is still years away.
- **Owning too many funds** that hold similar investments, which adds complexity without real diversification.
- **Investing money you will need soon** in volatile funds.
- **Ignoring costs.** Compare expense ratios within the same category.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think a SIP works best when it is linked to a clear goal and horizon, kept in a small number of funds you understand, and reviewed perhaps once a year rather than every day. Treat the SIP as a habit, and the fund choice as the decision that needs research.
</Callout>
