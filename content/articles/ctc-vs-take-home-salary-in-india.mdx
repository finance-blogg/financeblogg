**Cost to company (CTC)** is the employer’s estimate of what you cost them in a year. **Take-home** (in-hand) is what is credited after statutory deductions, tax and anything the employer holds back. Mixing the two is how people sign an offer that looks generous and then cannot cover rent.

This article unpacks a typical Indian private-sector structure. It is not your offer letter, and it does not calculate your tax.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Budget from **take-home**, not from CTC or even from “gross”.
- CTC often includes **employer PF**, gratuity provisions, insurance premiums and **variable pay** you may not receive.
- Employee PF, professional tax, ESI (if applicable) and **TDS** reduce the credit to your account.
- A joining bonus or annual variable paid once is not monthly cash.
- Use the payslip, not the offer PDF, as the input to a [monthly budget](/blog/how-to-create-a-monthly-budget-in-india).
</KeyTakeaways>

## What CTC usually contains

Employers package different things under CTC. Read the annexure, not the headline.

Common blocks:

- **Fixed pay:** basic, HRA, special allowance and other monthly components.
- **Retirals the employer pays:** the employer’s provident-fund contribution, and sometimes a gratuity provision.
- **Benefits:** group medical or term cover whose premium is counted as cost to the company.
- **Variable pay:** performance bonus, joining bonus, stock units shown at an assumed value.

Employer PF is money that may reach your EPFO account, but it is **not** in your salary credit. Variable pay is a target until it is paid. Equity is not cash until you can sell it under the plan rules.

## Gross, net and take-home

People use these words loosely. On many Indian payslips:

- **Gross** is the sum of monthly earnings before employee deductions.
- **Deductions** are employee PF, professional tax, ESI if it applies, TDS, and voluntary items such as extra insurance or food coupons.
- **Net pay** is what the bank transfer should match.

CTC is usually **annual**. Divide it by 12 only if you know every rupee in it is monthly cash. Often it is not.

<Callout type="example" title="Why CTC ÷ 12 is not your salary">
Illustrative only. Suppose CTC is shown as ₹12 lakh.

- Employer PF might be about one month of that package, sitting in EPFO, not in the bank.
- A 10% variable means ₹1.2 lakh is paid only if the company and you meet the plan’s conditions, often once a year.
- TDS depends on the remaining taxable income and the regime you choose.

The monthly credit can be several tens of thousands of rupees below ₹1 lakh even when the CTC looks like “a lakh a month”.
</Callout>

## Deductions that actually leave the payslip

**Employee provident fund.** Where EPF applies, a share of wages (subject to the wage ceiling the establishment uses) is deducted from you and a share is paid by the employer. The member passbook on EPFO’s site is the record, not a verbal HR summary.

**Professional tax.** A state levy in some states, usually a small monthly amount once income crosses that state’s threshold.

**ESI.** Applies only if the establishment and your wages fall under the Employees’ State Insurance Act. If it applies, both employee and employer contribute.

**Income tax (TDS).** The employer estimates annual tax and deducts it in instalments. The estimate is not your final tax. [Form 16](/blog/how-to-read-form-16) and [Form 26AS](/blog/how-to-read-form-26as-and-ais) are where you reconcile it.

<Callout type="verify">
Contribution rates, wage ceilings and professional-tax slabs are statutory and state-specific. Confirm on EPFO, ESIC and your state’s commercial-tax site, not from an old offer comparison on a forum.
</Callout>

## Components that look like pay but are not monthly cash

- **HRA** is part of taxable salary unless you actually pay rent and claim the exemption the law allows, in the regime that still permits it.
- **Reimbursements** (fuel, phones, meals) are cash only when you submit bills under the policy.
- **Meal or gift vouchers** reduce net pay if they are deducted, even if you value the voucher.
- **Gratuity** in CTC is usually a provision. Payment depends on the Payment of Gratuity Act and your service, not on the CTC line.

None of this is a reason to ignore the offer. It is a reason to build the household plan from the credit advice in the [bank statement](/blog/how-to-read-a-bank-statement).

## How to read an offer without fooling yourself

1. Split **fixed monthly cash**, **annual variable**, **retirals**, and **benefits**.
2. Ask whether variable is a target or a guaranteed amount, and in which month it is paid.
3. Ask whether PF is on basic only or on a larger wage, and whether the statutory ceiling is used.
4. Estimate TDS using the tax department’s calculator, or ask payroll for a sample payslip, rather than assuming zero tax.
5. Write the number that will probably hit the bank in a normal month. That is the input to [financial goals](/blog/how-to-set-financial-goals-in-india).

If two offers have the same CTC, the better one for cash flow is often the one with more **fixed pay** and fewer paper components.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think candidates should negotiate on fixed pay and on when variable is paid, not on a round CTC number. A higher CTC built from a joining bonus you must return if you leave, or from stock you cannot sell, does not pay next month’s rent.
</Callout>

## After you join

Download every payslip. Match net pay to the bank credit. If they differ, ask payroll before you assume a “bank delay”. Check that PF is appearing in the UAN passbook within a reasonable cycle.

When income changes, update the budget. CTC on LinkedIn is a social number. Households run on take-home.
