A savings account and a fixed deposit (FD) often sit side by side in the same bank app, and many people treat them as interchangeable places to "park" money. They are not. A savings account is built for **access**: you can move money in and out whenever you need to. A fixed deposit is built for **commitment**: you agree to leave money untouched for a period, and the bank usually pays a higher rate in return.

Knowing the difference helps you decide how much to keep in each, and avoid losing interest by breaking deposits early.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Savings accounts offer instant access and usually lower interest. FDs lock money for a fixed term and usually pay more.
- Breaking an FD early usually means a lower rate and a penalty.
- Interest from both is taxable at your slab rate. Banks deduct TDS on FD interest above a threshold.
- DICGC insures deposits up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank, **combined** across savings, FDs and other deposits at that bank.
- Many people use both: a savings account for spending and short-term needs, FDs for money they will not need for a known period.
</KeyTakeaways>

## How a savings account works

A savings account holds money you may need at short notice. You can withdraw through UPI, ATMs, net banking or at the branch, and there is no fixed term.

**Interest.** Banks set their own savings account rates. Interest is calculated on the balance you hold at the end of each day, and banks typically credit it to your account at regular intervals, such as quarterly. Some banks offer higher rates on larger balances, so the rate you see advertised may apply only above a certain amount.

**Minimum balance.** Many savings accounts require you to maintain an average balance and charge a fee if you fall short. Basic Savings Bank Deposit Accounts (BSBDA) do not require a minimum balance but come with some limits on services. Check your bank's schedule of charges.

## How a fixed deposit works

With an FD, you deposit a lump sum for a fixed period, which can range from a few days to several years, at a rate that is fixed when you open it. The rate does not change during the term, even if the bank later changes its rates for new deposits.

You usually choose between:

- **Cumulative FDs**, where interest is added to the deposit and paid at maturity. Interest compounds, typically quarterly.
- **Non-cumulative FDs**, where interest is paid out monthly, quarterly or yearly. These suit people who want a regular income, such as retirees.

Banks often offer slightly higher rates to senior citizens. Some banks also offer **tax-saving FDs** with a five-year lock-in, which qualify for a deduction only under the old tax regime.

**Breaking an FD early.** Most FDs allow premature withdrawal, but the bank will usually pay interest at the rate that applied for the period you actually held the deposit, minus a penalty set by the bank. Some deposits are "non-callable" and cannot be withdrawn early at all. Read the terms before you open one.

## Side-by-side comparison

| Feature | Savings account | Fixed deposit |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Access to money | Any time | At maturity; early withdrawal usually penalised |
| Interest rate | Usually lower; can change at any time | Usually higher; fixed for the term |
| Interest calculation | Daily balance, credited periodically | Compounded (cumulative) or paid out (non-cumulative) |
| Best for | Spending money, short-term needs, part of an emergency fund | Money not needed for a known period |
| Tax on interest | Taxable at slab rate | Taxable at slab rate; TDS above a threshold |
| Deposit insurance | Covered by DICGC within the combined limit | Covered by DICGC within the combined limit |

## Tax on interest

Interest from both savings accounts and FDs is added to your income and taxed at your slab rate. It is not tax-free just because it comes from a bank.

**TDS on FD interest.** Banks deduct tax at source (TDS) on interest from deposits once the interest you earn from that bank in a year crosses a threshold, which is higher for senior citizens. If your total income is below the taxable limit, you may be eligible to submit **Form 15G** (or **Form 15H** if you are a senior citizen) so that the bank does not deduct TDS. TDS is not an extra tax; it is credited against your final tax liability and shows in your Form 26AS and Annual Information Statement.

**Savings interest deduction.** Under the old tax regime, individuals have been able to claim a deduction for a limited amount of savings account interest each year, with a higher limit for senior citizens covering deposit interest too. These deductions are not available under the new tax regime, which is now the default.

<Callout type="verify">
TDS thresholds, deduction limits and the section numbers that contain them change through the annual Finance Act, and India's Income-tax Act, 2025 replaced the 1961 Act from 1 April 2026 with renumbered sections. Check the current rules on the Income Tax Department website before relying on any specific figure.
</Callout>

## Deposit insurance: how safe is your money?

Deposits at banks in India, including savings accounts, current accounts, FDs and recurring deposits, are insured by the **Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation (DICGC)**, a subsidiary of the RBI. The cover is up to **₹5 lakh per depositor per bank**, including both principal and interest.

Two details matter:

1. The limit is **combined** across all your deposits at the same bank in the same capacity. A savings account with ₹2 lakh and an FD with ₹4 lakh at one bank are insured up to ₹5 lakh in total, not ₹6 lakh.
2. Deposits at **different banks** are insured separately. Spreading large sums across banks increases the amount covered.

Deposits with non-banking financial companies (NBFCs) and company fixed deposits are **not** covered by DICGC.

## A worked example

<Callout type="example" title="₹1 lakh for one year">
The rates below are invented for illustration and are not current market rates. Check your bank's website for actual rates.

- **Savings account at 3% a year**: about ₹3,000 of interest over the year if the balance stays at ₹1 lakh.
- **One-year cumulative FD at 7% a year, compounded quarterly**: ₹1,00,000 × (1 + 0.07 ÷ 4)⁴ ≈ ₹1,07,186, so about ₹7,186 of interest.

Both amounts are taxable. Someone in the 30% slab would pay roughly 31% of the interest as tax once the 4% cess is included, leaving about ₹2,060 from the savings account and about ₹4,940 from the FD.

If that person broke the FD after six months, the bank might pay the six-month rate minus a penalty, which could be well below 7%.
</Callout>

The example shows the trade-off clearly. The FD earns more, but only if you can genuinely leave the money alone for the full term.

## Using both together

Most households benefit from a mix:

- **Savings account**: your monthly spending money, plus a portion of your emergency fund that you may need immediately.
- **Fixed deposits**: money you will not need for a known period, such as funds set aside for a fee due in a year, or the less-urgent part of an emergency fund.

Two practical techniques help:

- **FD laddering.** Instead of one large FD, open several smaller ones maturing at different times, for example every three or six months. If you need cash, you break only one small deposit, and you regularly have money maturing.
- **Sweep-in or auto-sweep accounts.** Some banks automatically move balances above a set level into FDs, and break them in small units when your savings balance falls short. Rules vary by bank, so check how interest is calculated when a sweep FD is broken.

Our guide to [emergency funds](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save) explains how much to keep accessible.

## Limitations to keep in mind

- **Inflation.** After tax, the interest on savings accounts and FDs may not keep pace with rising prices. That is acceptable for money you need to keep safe and available, but it matters for long-term goals. See [how inflation affects your savings](/blog/how-inflation-affects-your-savings).
- **Reinvestment risk.** When an FD matures, rates may be lower than when you opened it.
- **Higher rates come with conditions.** An unusually high advertised rate may apply only to a specific tenure, a minimum amount or a particular customer group. With smaller or less-known banks, check that the institution is a bank covered by DICGC.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think the right question is not "which is better" but "when will I need this money?" Money you might need this month belongs in a savings account. Money you are confident you will not touch for a set period can usually earn more in an FD.
</Callout>
