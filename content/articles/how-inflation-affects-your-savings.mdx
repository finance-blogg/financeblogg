If a packet of groceries costs ₹1,000 today and ₹1,060 a year from now, your money has lost some of its purchasing power, even though the number in your bank account has not changed. That is inflation. It works slowly and quietly, which is exactly why it is easy to ignore and important to understand.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Inflation is the general rise in prices over time. In India, it is commonly measured by the **Consumer Price Index (CPI)**.
- What matters for your savings is the **real return**: the return after inflation, and ideally after tax.
- Even modest inflation roughly halves purchasing power over a couple of decades.
- Money for short-term needs should prioritise safety, even if its real return is low. Long-term goals need a plan that accounts for inflation.
- Your personal inflation rate can differ from the official figure.
</KeyTakeaways>

## What inflation is and how India measures it

Inflation is the rate at which the general level of prices rises. In India, the most widely followed measure is **CPI inflation**, published monthly by the National Statistics Office under the Ministry of Statistics and Programme Implementation. The CPI tracks the prices of a basket of goods and services that households typically buy, including food, housing, clothing, fuel, health, education and transport.

The Reserve Bank of India uses CPI inflation as the main target for monetary policy. Under India's flexible inflation-targeting framework, the target is **4% CPI inflation**, with a tolerance band of 2% to 6%. The RBI's Monetary Policy Committee adjusts the policy repo rate partly to keep inflation within this range.

## Nominal vs real return

The **nominal return** is the headline rate: the interest an FD pays, or the percentage gain on an investment. The **real return** is what is left after inflation, and it tells you whether your money is actually buying more over time.

A quick approximation is:

**Real return ≈ nominal return − inflation**

The precise formula is (1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation) − 1, but the approximation is close enough for most everyday comparisons.

<Callout type="example" title="Real return on a fixed deposit">
The rates below are illustrative, not current figures.

- FD interest: 7% a year
- Inflation: 5% a year
- Approximate real return: 7% − 5% = **2%**
- Precise real return: 1.07 ÷ 1.05 − 1 ≈ **1.9%**
</Callout>

## Don't forget tax

Interest from deposits is taxed at your slab rate, which lowers the return further.

<Callout type="example" title="After tax and inflation">
Continuing the example above, for someone in the 30% tax slab, with 4% cess, the effective tax rate on interest is about 31.2%.

- After-tax FD return: 7% × (1 − 0.312) ≈ 4.8%
- Inflation: 5%
- Approximate real, after-tax return: about **−0.2%**

In this scenario, the deposit keeps the money safe and available but slightly loses purchasing power each year.
</Callout>

This is not an argument against deposits. For money you need in the near future, safety and access are worth more than a higher return. It does mean that **long-term goals funded only from deposits** may need much larger savings than people expect.

## How inflation compounds over time

The effect of inflation grows with time, just like interest.

<Callout type="example" title="₹1 lakh of today's expenses">
Assuming a constant **illustrative** inflation rate of 6% a year:

- in 10 years, the same things would cost about ₹1.79 lakh;
- in 20 years, about ₹3.21 lakh.

Put the other way, ₹1 lakh kept as cash would buy only about a third as much after 20 years.
</Callout>

A handy shortcut is the **rule of 72**: divide 72 by the inflation rate to estimate how many years it takes for prices to double, or for the purchasing power of cash to halve. At 6% inflation, that is about 72 ÷ 6 = 12 years.

## Your personal inflation rate

The official CPI is an average across many households. Your own inflation rate depends on what **you** spend on. For example:

- families paying school or college fees may see education costs rise faster than the average;
- older households may spend a larger share on healthcare;
- a household that rents in a city where rents are rising will feel that more than one that owns its home.

When planning for a specific goal, such as a child's education, use an estimate that reflects that cost rather than the headline CPI. Treat any such estimate as an assumption to revisit, not a certainty.

## Matching savings to time horizons

A practical way to think about inflation is to sort your money by **when you will need it**:

| Time horizon | Main priority | Inflation's role |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Under about 3 years, including emergency funds | Safety and access | Accept a low real return; protecting the money matters more |
| About 3 to 7 years | Balance of stability and growth | Consider options that may beat inflation, while limiting the risk of a large fall near the goal |
| Over about 7 years | Growth that outpaces inflation | Inflation does the most damage here, so the plan has to account for it |

These ranges are rough guides, not rules. Investments with the potential to beat inflation over long periods, such as equity, also come with the risk of significant falls in value along the way, and no investment guarantees a positive real return. ## Practical steps

1. **Separate short-term and long-term money.** Keep your [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save) and near-term savings safe. Plan long-term goals separately.
2. **Estimate goals in future rupees.** If a goal costs ₹10 lakh today and is ten years away, its future cost could be considerably higher.
3. **Compare returns after tax and inflation**, not just headline rates.
4. **Review your plan each year**, as inflation, rates and your circumstances change.
5. **Diversify** rather than relying on a single type of asset.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think the most useful habit inflation teaches is to ask "what will this buy?" rather than "how much will this be?" A goal measured in future purchasing power is much easier to plan for honestly.
</Callout>
