An **index fund** is a mutual fund scheme that aims to **mirror a stated market index** (for example a broad equity or debt benchmark), rather than picking securities to “beat” that index. In India, index funds are offered under SEBI’s mutual fund framework and are often described as **passive** strategies.

This explainer is educational. It is not a recommendation to buy any scheme.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Index funds seek to **track** a benchmark’s composition and returns, before costs.
- They still carry **market risk**—if the index falls, the fund can fall.
- Compare **TER**, tracking information and the **exact index** named on the [factsheet](/blog/how-to-read-a-mutual-fund-factsheet).
- Passive does not mean “set and forget without reading documents”.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Active vs passive, briefly

Many mutual funds try to outperform a benchmark after fees through research and security selection (**active**). An index fund instead holds a portfolio designed to **replicate** the index (or a close sampling), accepting that its job is tracking, not outperformance.

Neither label automatically means “safer” or “better”. They answer different design goals.

## What “tracking an index” means

The scheme documents name the index. The fund manager’s mandate is to keep the portfolio aligned so that fund returns move closely with that index over time, subject to costs, cash drag, corporate actions and replication method.

Two related ideas appear in education materials:

- **Tracking error** — variability of the difference between fund and index returns.  
- **Tracking difference** — how far cumulative fund returns sit from the index over a period (often after expenses).

You do not need to become a statistician; you do need to know that **perfect match every day is not the promise**.

## Costs still matter

Even passive funds charge a [Total Expense Ratio (TER)](/blog/mutual-fund-expense-ratio-explained-india). Because the strategy is not trying to outperform by a wide margin, costs can be a larger share of the gap versus the index. Direct and regular plans can differ in expense—see [direct vs regular plans](/blog/direct-vs-regular-mutual-fund-plans).

<Callout type="example" title="Illustrative idea only">
If an index returned a certain percentage in a year and a fund tracking it returned slightly less, part of the gap may be expenses and friction. Real numbers belong on the latest factsheet, not in a remembered tip.
</Callout>

## What to read on the factsheet

1. **SEBI category** and scheme name  
2. **Underlying index** (exact name)  
3. **Riskometer** and suitable investor description in offer documents  
4. **TER** for the plan you are viewing  
5. **Exit load**, if any  
6. Portfolio / replication notes when provided  

For a full walkthrough of factsheet lines, use our [factsheet guide](/blog/how-to-read-a-mutual-fund-factsheet). For SIP mechanics that also apply to index funds, see [SIP and mutual fund investing](/blog/understanding-sip-and-mutual-fund-investing).

## Myths about index funds

- “Index funds cannot lose money.” They can.  
- “One index fund fits every goal.” Goals, horizons and asset mix still matter ([asset allocation basics](/blog/what-asset-allocation-means-for-beginners)).  
- “Lowest TER is the only decision.” Tracking quality, index choice and your behaviour also matter.  
- “Passive means no documents.” KIM and scheme documents still apply.

## How households typically use the idea (education, not advice)

Some learners use broad equity index funds as a way to understand market returns without studying individual stocks. Others compare index and active schemes in the same category as a learning exercise. Suitability depends on risk capacity, horizon and overall finances—including emergency savings and debt—not on a slogan.

<Callout type="verify">
Index methodologies, scheme portfolios and regulatory disclosures change. Confirm the current index definition and scheme documents on AMC/AMFI/SEBI materials before relying on an old article or video.
</Callout>

## Bottom line

An index fund is a **rules-based mirror** of a published benchmark, minus real-world frictions. Learn to name the index, read costs and accept market risk. That literacy is the goal—not a tip to switch everything today.
