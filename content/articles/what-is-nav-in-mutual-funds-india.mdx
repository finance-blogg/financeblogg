**NAV (Net Asset Value)** is the per-unit value of a mutual fund scheme’s portfolio after accounting for scheme liabilities and expenses as per applicable norms. When you invest, you are generally allotted **units**; when you redeem, units are cancelled at the applicable NAV for that transaction under SEBI/AMFI process rules.

This article explains NAV conceptually. It is not a tip to buy any scheme because its NAV “looks low.”

<KeyTakeaways>
- NAV is the **scheme’s net assets ÷ outstanding units** (simplified).
- A **lower NAV is not a discount**; unit count adjusts with NAV.
- You use NAV to understand **transaction value**, not to rank “cheap vs expensive” funds.
- Read NAV beside [expense ratio](/blog/mutual-fund-expense-ratio-explained-india), [factsheet](/blog/how-to-read-a-mutual-fund-factsheet), and risk disclosures.
</KeyTakeaways>

## The simple formula (intuition)

At a high level:

**NAV per unit ≈ (Market value of investments + other assets − liabilities − expenses as applicable) ÷ number of outstanding units**

Professionals follow valuation and expense rules set under the mutual fund regulatory framework. As a beginner, you mainly need this intuition: if the portfolio rises, NAV tends to rise; if it falls, NAV tends to fall—**before** your own behaviour (buying/selling) and costs.

## Units make NAV comparable across time for *you*

Suppose you invest a fixed rupee amount. At a higher NAV you receive fewer units; at a lower NAV you receive more units. Your wealth is roughly **units × NAV** (subject to loads/exit loads and taxes as applicable)—not the NAV number alone.

That is why “this fund’s NAV is only ₹12 so it will double easily, unlike a ₹250 NAV fund” is a classic beginner error. Two schemes can hold similar securities with very different NAVs purely because of history of unit creation and corporate actions in the scheme’s life.

<Callout type="example" title="Illustrative unit maths">
Investment of ₹10,000 at NAV ₹20 → about 500 units. The same ₹10,000 at NAV ₹100 → about 100 units. If both portfolios then rise 10% before costs, both positions are worth about ₹11,000. NAV level did not create a bargain. Figures are illustrative only.
</Callout>

## When NAV is declared

Open-ended schemes typically declare NAV for each business day under applicable rules. The **NAV applicable to your purchase or redemption** depends on when your transaction is accepted relative to **cut-off timings** and fund category (for example liquid vs others)—details that AMCs publish and that can be updated.

Do not assume the NAV you see on a website at lunchtime is automatically the NAV for a request you place at night.

<Callout type="verify">
Cut-off timings, applicable NAV rules, and valuation practices are governed by SEBI regulations and operational circulars and can change. Confirm on the AMC’s official pages, AMFI (amfiindia.com), and investor.sebi.gov.in.
</Callout>

## NAV vs returns vs “price”

- **NAV** — today’s per-unit net asset value  
- **Returns** — how NAV (with dividends/reinvestment assumptions as disclosed) moved over a period  
- **Market price of a stock** — not the same object as a mutual fund NAV  

For index funds, NAV should track the index reasonably closely after costs—see [what is an index fund](/blog/what-is-an-index-fund-in-india). Tracking difference and [TER](/blog/mutual-fund-expense-ratio-explained-india) matter more than whether NAV is ₹45 or ₹145.

## Practical uses of NAV for households

1. **Confirm allotment** — units × allotment NAV should reconcile with the amount invested (after disclosed charges).  
2. **Track SIPs** — each instalment may buy units at different NAVs; that is normal [rupee-cost averaging](/blog/what-is-rupee-cost-averaging) behaviour, not an error.  
3. **Read factsheets** — performance tables are built from NAV history; learn [how to read a factsheet](/blog/how-to-read-a-mutual-fund-factsheet).  
4. **Ignore “cheap NAV” tips** — focus on objective, risk, costs, and suitability instead.

## What NAV does not tell you

- Whether a fund is “safe”  
- Whether an AMC is “better”  
- Whether you should switch schemes this week  
- Your personal tax on redemption (depends on holding period, type of fund, and tax law)

For process literacy on investing mode, see [SIP and mutual fund investing](/blog/understanding-sip-and-mutual-fund-investing) and [direct vs regular plans](/blog/direct-vs-regular-mutual-fund-plans).

## Bottom line

NAV is the **meter reading** of a mutual fund’s per-unit net assets. Use it to understand unit allotment and performance arithmetic. Do not use a low NAV as evidence of a bargain—or a high NAV as evidence of quality.
