A **factsheet** is the monthly (sometimes quarterly) snapshot an asset management company publishes for each scheme: where the money sits, what it costs, how risky the label says it is, and how the scheme behaved in the past. It is not a recommendation, and it is not the full legal document — that is the scheme information document (SID) and the key information memorandum (KIM).

This guide is about reading the factsheet so you are not sold by a single return number. It does not pick a fund.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Start with **SEBI category**, **benchmark** and **riskometer**, not with the 1-year return.
- **TER** (and direct vs regular) is a continuing drag; [direct and regular plans](/blog/direct-vs-regular-mutual-fund-plans) explain why two NAVs exist.
- The **portfolio** tells you what you actually own; the scheme name is marketing.
- **Exit load** and lock-in change when you can leave without a haircut.
- Past returns are **backward**. They are not a schedule of future EMIs from the market.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Where to get the real file

Download the factsheet from the **AMC website** or from a registrar/platform that hosts the official PDF, for the **same plan** you hold or might buy (direct growth vs regular growth are different NAVs). Date-stamp it. A screenshot from a tip account is not a document.

For definitions of NAV, SIP and categories, see [how SIPs and mutual funds work](/blog/understanding-sip-and-mutual-fund-investing).

## Identity lines

**Scheme name and SEBI category.** Large-cap, flexi-cap, short-duration debt, conservative hybrid — these are regulatory boxes so you can compare like with like. A “banking opportunities” story inside a sectoral box is not a flexi-cap.

**Benchmark.** The index the scheme is measured against. A large-cap fund beating a small-cap index is the wrong exam.

**Plan and option.** Growth vs IDCW (income distribution). IDCW is not a guaranteed rent. Direct vs regular changes the TER.

**Inception date and AUM.** Very new schemes have little history. Very large AUM in a small-cap strategy can become a liquidity issue; the factsheet will not always shout that.

## Cost and liquidity

**Total expense ratio.** Annual cost, charged inside the NAV. Compare TER **inside the same category and plan**. SEBI caps the maximum; funds can charge less.

**Exit load.** A percentage if you redeem before a stated period. Factor this into any money you might need early.

**Minimum SIP/lumpsum.** Operational, not a quality signal.

<Callout type="note">
TER is deducted continuously. A “zero commission” pitch on a regular plan is still a regular plan if the TER is the regular TER. Read the number, not the banner.
</Callout>

## Riskometer and portfolio

SEBI’s **riskometer** is a six-level pictorial risk label based on the scheme’s portfolio, reviewed periodically. It can move. It is not insurance against loss.

Then read **top holdings**, **sector weights**, and for debt funds **duration, yield, and credit-quality tables**.

Questions to ask:

- Is this equity fund quietly a mid- and small-cap book with a calm name?
- Do I already own these same stocks in another folio ([diversification](/blog/what-diversification-means-for-beginner-investors) vs clutter)?
- For debt: is the yield coming from longer duration or weaker credit?

## Performance tables without fooling yourself

Factsheets show returns for 6 months, 1, 3, 5 years, since inception, and often a SIP illustration.

Read them with three filters:

1. **Same category, same period, versus benchmark and versus a simple index fund in that box.**
2. **Point-to-point luck.** A 3-year number that starts at a market bottom looks heroic.
3. **The SIP table uses a past path.** It is not a promise that your SIP will match it.

Standard deviation, Sharpe ratios and “alpha” appear on some sheets. If you cannot explain them, skip them; they do not make a risky book safe.

<Callout type="example" title="Two ways to misread 18%">
Illustrative only. A factsheet shows 18% for the last year.

- If the category average was 17% and the benchmark 16%, you learned little except that the year was strong for the whole box.
- If the category is short-duration debt and 18% came from credit events going well, you did not find an FD substitute.

Never treat a factsheet return as an [EMI](/blog/how-loan-emi-is-calculated) you can spend.
</Callout>

## Turnover, tracking error, and manager notes

**Portfolio turnover** hints how often the fund trades (costs sit in performance). **Tracking error** matters for index funds: how tightly they follow the index. The fund manager’s commentary is opinion. The holdings table is closer to fact.

## What the factsheet will not tell you

- Whether *you* should own this for a goal next year.
- Tax on your redemption or IDCW (depends on your situation and the year’s law).
- How you will behave in a 30% fall.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think one evening with three factsheets in the **same SEBI category** beats a month of reels. If you cannot tell the three portfolios apart, you do not need all three.
</Callout>

## A 10-minute checklist

1. Category, benchmark, plan (direct/regular, growth/IDCW).
2. Riskometer and date of factsheet.
3. TER and exit load.
4. Top 10 holdings and overlap with what you already own.
5. Debt: duration and credit mix, if it is a debt scheme.
6. Returns vs benchmark for more than one period — then close the PDF and look at your [goal horizon](/blog/how-to-set-financial-goals-in-india).

If the SID and factsheet disagree, the SID wins. If a seller disagrees with both, walk away.
