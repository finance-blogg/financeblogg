A health policy can look like it covers “hospitalisation” in the brochure and still pay nothing for the illness you actually have, in the month you actually have it. Two reasons sit in the wording: **waiting periods** (not yet) and **exclusions** (not this, not ever, under this policy).

This article explains those clocks and lists. Use it with [how to compare health policies](/blog/how-to-compare-health-insurance-policies). It is not a product recommendation and it is not your policy schedule.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A **waiting period** is time after the policy starts (or after a specific cover starts) during which that cover does not pay.
- **Pre-existing disease (PED)** waiting is usually the longest clock that matters if you already have a condition.
- An **exclusion** is a claim the policy will not pay even after waiting periods, unless a specific rider says otherwise.
- Porting a policy can carry **waiting-period credit**; buying a new one often restarts clocks.
- The **policy wording and schedule** beat the app tile and the agent’s summary.
</KeyTakeaways>

## The clocks you will actually meet

Names vary by insurer, but the idea repeats.

**Initial waiting period.** A short period (often around 30 days) after first purchase when illness hospitalisation is not covered, with an exception for accidents in many products. It exists so people do not buy a policy on the way to the hospital.

**Specified-disease waiting.** Named procedures or illnesses (for example, some hernia, cataract or joint procedures — the list is in *your* wording) wait longer, often months to a couple of years.

**Pre-existing disease waiting.** Conditions you already had, as defined in the policy, wait the longest. Disclosure on the proposal form is how the insurer knows; non-disclosure is how claims are denied.

**Maternity, mental health, OPDs, and restoration** each have their own waiting or sub-limits if they exist at all.

<Callout type="verify">
IRDAI has standardised many health-product features and capped some waiting periods over successive regulations. Your policy may be shorter than an old blog’s “four years for PED”. Read the current wording and the policy year on the schedule.
</Callout>

## Exclusions are not waiting periods

After every clock has run, the policy can still exclude:

- specific treatments or substances;
- participation in certain activities;
- costs the product never promised (some dental, some vision, some outpatient);
- hospitalisation that does not meet the definition (hours, registered hospital, medical necessity);
- charges that hit [room-rent limits and proportionate deduction](/blog/how-to-compare-health-insurance-policies).

A waiting period **expires**. An exclusion **does not**, unless you buy a different cover or the insurer issues an endorsement.

## PED and the proposal form

A pre-existing disease is not “whatever the claims team dislikes”. It is defined in the policy, typically around conditions diagnosed or treated in a look-back period before inception.

If you hide a diagnosis to get a cheaper premium, you have not outsmarted the waiting period. You have created a **non-disclosure** problem. If you declare it, you may get a loading, an exclusion, or a PED wait — unpleasant, but the contract is then honest.

<Callout type="example" title="Same diagnosis, different year">
Illustrative only. You buy a policy in April.

- A road accident in May: many policies treat accidents as outside the initial illness wait.
- A planned knee surgery on a condition documented last year: likely sitting behind a specified or PED wait, not behind “hospitalisation is covered”.
- The same knee surgery after the PED wait has expired, with the condition declared: then you still check sub-limits, network, and exclusions.
</Callout>

## Portability and waiting-period credit

IRDAI’s portability framework exists so you can move to another insurer without always restarting every wait, **if** you apply in time and the new insurer accepts the port on those terms. Credit is not automatic for every add-on. A break in cover usually breaks the credit.

Do not drop an old policy before the new one is in force with waiting-period mapping in writing.

## Day-one marketing vs day-of-claim

Ads emphasise “cashless” and “no room-rent limit”. Claims teams emphasise:

- was the illness in waiting?
- was it excluded?
- was the hospital in network **for that product**?
- were documents filed in time?

Keep discharge summaries, test reports, and the proposal copy. For a parallel on reading boring documents, [Form 16](/blog/how-to-read-form-16) is the tax version of the same habit.

## What to check in 20 minutes

1. Initial wait, specified-disease list and PED wait, in the wording.
2. Permanent exclusions and standard exclusions annexure.
3. Sub-limits: disease-wise caps, implants, cataract, joint replacement.
4. Room rent / proportionate deduction.
5. Whether last year’s conditions were **declared**.
6. Portability credit, if this is not your first policy.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think the most expensive health-insurance mistake is buying only for the tax invoice or the app badge, then discovering the wait when a parent is already admitted. Compare clocks before premium. Premium is the easy number.
</Callout>

## If a claim is rejected

Read the rejection letter against the wording. Ask for the clause. Use the insurer’s grievance officer, then IRDAI’s Bima Bharosa facility if needed. A social-media complaint is not a regulatory filing.

Waiting periods are not a scandal by themselves. Undisclosed clocks are. Put them on the same list as [term life](/blog/how-term-life-insurance-works-in-india) tenure: a feature you must see before you pay.
