Income and expenses tell you what happened this month. **Net worth** tells you where you stand overall: what you own minus what you owe. Tracking it once or twice a year is enough for most households. This is a learning guide, not a product recommendation.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Net worth = **assets − liabilities** at a chosen date.
- Include cash, bank deposits, investments you can value, and property you own; include loans and credit-card dues as liabilities.
- Use the **same date** and the **same method** each time so the trend is meaningful.
- A rising net worth can still hide a thin [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save); track cash buffers separately.
</KeyTakeaways>

## What net worth is (and is not)

Net worth is a snapshot. It is not your salary, not your credit score, and not a forecast of future wealth. Two people with the same take-home pay can have very different net worth depending on savings, debt and asset prices.

It is also not advice to sell a house or redeem investments. It is a measurement tool.

## List assets first

Write down what you own and can reasonably value:

- Cash and balances in [savings accounts](/blog/savings-account-vs-fixed-deposit), current accounts and cash at home.
- Fixed deposits, recurring deposits and similar bank deposits.
- Mutual fund units, stocks, bonds or other market investments (use a recent statement or app value).
- Employee benefits you can assign a clear balance to (for example, EPF), if you include them consistently every year.
- Property you own (use a conservative estimate, or cost, and stick to one method).
- Vehicles and other saleable items only if you want a fuller picture—many people skip them to keep the sheet simple.

Skip “hoped-for” bonuses and informal IOUs you may never collect.

## Then list liabilities

Write down what you owe:

- Home loan, car loan, education loan and personal loan outstanding balances.
- Credit-card outstanding (the full amount due, not only a minimum payment).
- Borrowings from friends or family that you intend to repay.
- Other dues that are clearly your obligation.

Do not subtract “mental” debt you have not actually taken.

<Callout type="example" title="Illustrative household snapshot">
On 31 March, Meera lists ₹4 lakh in bank deposits, ₹6 lakh in mutual funds, and a flat she values at ₹40 lakh for tracking. She owes ₹28 lakh on a home loan and ₹40,000 on a credit card. Assets ₹50 lakh − liabilities ₹28.4 lakh = net worth ₹21.6 lakh. The numbers are illustrative only.
</Callout>

## How often to update

For most salaried households, **once or twice a year** is enough—say after filing taxes or at the financial year end. Monthly tracking is optional if you enjoy it; it is not required for good money habits.

Use the same spreadsheet or notebook page. Change the date, refresh balances, and save a copy so you can see the trend.

## Mistakes that distort the picture

- Mixing **cost** and **market value** randomly across years.
- Ignoring credit-card dues while counting every small asset.
- Counting a parent’s property you do not own.
- Treating a rising mutual fund value as “extra spending money”.
- Comparing your sheet with someone else’s social media lifestyle.

## Link net worth to goals

Net worth supports [goal setting](/blog/how-to-set-financial-goals-in-india): debt reduction, emergency savings and long-term investing all show up here over time. Pair it with a simple [monthly budget](/blog/how-to-create-a-monthly-budget-in-india) so cash flow and the balance sheet stay connected.
