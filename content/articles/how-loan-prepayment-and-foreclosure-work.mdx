An EMI is a schedule, not a lock. Most loans let you pay extra, or close the account before the last due date. That can cut the interest you pay over the life of the loan. It can also trigger fees, change your tax deductions, or leave you short of cash you later need.

This article explains the mechanics. It does not tell you whether to prepay your loan.

<KeyTakeaways>
- **Prepayment** is an extra payment while the loan continues. **Foreclosure** (or closure) pays the entire outstanding and ends the loan.
- Extra payments reduce **principal**. Interest is calculated on what is still owed, so less principal means less future interest.
- You usually choose a **shorter tenure** or a **lower EMI** after a part-prepayment. Ask which one the lender will apply.
- Charges depend on the **loan type, fixed vs floating rate, and the sanction letter**. Do not assume every loan is free to close.
- Keep an [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save) before you empty savings into a loan.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Prepayment vs foreclosure

**Part-prepayment** is a lump sum, or a series of extra amounts, paid on top of EMIs. The loan number stays open. You still have an outstanding principal, and EMIs continue unless you ask for a change.

**Foreclosure** (sometimes called pre-closure or full prepayment) pays whatever is still owed, plus any fees the contract allows, and the lender issues a closure letter and, for a secured loan, releases the security.

A third option is **EMI overpayment**: rounding up the monthly debit. That is still principal reduction if the lender applies the extra to principal rather than parking it as an advance EMI. Confirm in writing.

## Why extra payments change the interest bill

Interest for a typical reducing-balance loan is charged on the **outstanding principal**. Pay principal down, and the next month’s interest is calculated on a smaller base. That is why a prepayment early in a long loan can save more interest than the same rupee amount paid near the end, when most of each EMI is already principal.

Read [how loan interest rates work](/blog/understanding-loan-interest-rates) if the difference between flat and reducing-balance rates is still unclear.

<Callout type="example" title="Same ₹1 lakh, different timing">
Figures below are simplified and illustrative.

Suppose ₹8 lakh is outstanding on a floating-rate loan. You receive ₹1 lakh.

- Applied as **part-prepayment**, outstanding becomes ₹7 lakh. Future interest is charged on ₹7 lakh.
- Held as **cash** for a known expense next month, the loan is unchanged, but you avoid a new, more expensive loan later.

The cheaper path depends on the loan’s rate, any prepayment fee, tax treatment, and whether you would otherwise borrow again. There is no universal answer.
</Callout>

## Tenure vs EMI after a part-prepayment

Lenders typically offer one of two resets:

- **Keep the EMI, shorten the tenure.** You become debt-free sooner. The monthly cash flow stays the same.
- **Keep the tenure, lower the EMI.** Monthly surplus rises. You stay in the loan for the original years unless you prepay again.

If your goal is to cut total interest, shortening tenure usually does more of the work, because you spend fewer months paying interest. If your goal is breathing room in the monthly budget, a lower EMI may be the point. Ask the lender which default they use, and get the revised schedule.

## Charges: read the contract, then the current rule

RBI has restricted foreclosure and pre-payment charges on several categories of **floating-rate** loans to **individual** borrowers, especially in retail lending. Many home loans on a floating rate can be prepaid without a penalty. That is not a blanket rule for every product.

Charges may still appear, depending on the lender and the product, for example:

- some **fixed-rate** loans;
- loans that are not to an individual, or not in a category the direction covers;
- lock-in clauses in the sanction letter;
- administrative or statement fees that are not labelled “prepayment penalty” but still cost money.

<Callout type="verify">
Fair-practice and charges rules are updated through RBI circulars and master directions. Before you pay a large sum, read your sanction letter, the latest charges schedule on the lender’s website, and the current RBI direction. A relationship manager’s verbal “no problem” is not a document.
</Callout>

Ask, in writing:

1. Outstanding principal as of a named date.
2. Interest from that date until the money is applied.
3. Any foreclosure or part-prepayment fee, GST on the fee, and lock-in remaining.
4. Whether the extra amount will reduce tenure or EMI.
5. How many days the payment takes to reflect, especially if you are transferring from another bank.

## Home loans, tax and offsets

On a home loan, interest you pay may be eligible for a deduction, subject to the tax regime you use and the current law. Prepaying reduces future interest, which can reduce that deduction. That does not make prepayment a bad idea; it means the comparison is **after-tax cost of the loan** versus what the same money would earn elsewhere, with risk included.

Our [income tax basics](/blog/basics-of-income-tax-in-india) explainer covers how deductions differ across regimes. Confirm the current sections and caps before you treat a deduction as a reason to stay in debt.

An **offset** or overdraft-linked home loan, where idle savings reduce the principal on which interest is charged, is a different product. Do not assume your ordinary home loan works that way.

## Personal loans and credit cards

Unsecured personal loans and outstanding credit-card balances often cost more than home loans. Prepaying the expensive balance first is a common approach because the interest rate difference is large. Credit-card revolving interest is not the same as a reducing-balance EMI; paying more than the minimum is usually the first job.

Foreclosing a personal loan can still involve a fee. Compare the fee with the remaining interest. A loan with three EMIs left and a 4% foreclosure charge can be a poor close.

Late payments and frequent closures can still show up in your [credit information](/blog/how-credit-scores-work-in-india). Closing a loan by paying it is not a negative event by itself; missing EMIs while you wait to prepay is.

## Do not prepay the emergency fund

Using the last of your savings to close a cheap, floating-rate home loan can leave you taking a personal loan at a much higher rate when the water tank bursts. Keep a cash buffer. Then decide whether extra money should reduce debt, sit in a [recurring deposit](/blog/how-recurring-deposits-work), or wait for a dated goal.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think prepayment is a maths-and-cash-flow decision, not a moral one. Being EMI-free feels good. Paying 8% to clear a loan while you later borrow at 16% feels expensive. Run both numbers, keep the closure letter, and do not skip the buffer.
</Callout>

After foreclosure, download the **loan closure letter**, check that any ECS or NACH mandate is cancelled, and, for a home loan, follow up until the original documents are released and the charge on the property is removed. The last step is paperwork, and it is part of the job.
