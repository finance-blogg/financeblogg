Most financial setbacks do not come from one dramatic decision. They come from small, common habits that quietly compound over years: a credit card balance carried month to month, an insurance policy bought for the wrong reason, a nomination never updated.

None of the mistakes below are unusual, and avoiding them does not require expertise. Each comes with a practical alternative.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Build an emergency fund before taking on investment risk.
- Separate insurance from investment, and make sure your health and life cover is adequate.
- Pay credit card bills in full; the minimum due is not the amount you owe.
- Be very cautious of tips, "guaranteed" returns and unregistered advisers.
- Keep nominations and records up to date so your family is not left searching.
</KeyTakeaways>

## 1. Not having an emergency fund

Without a cash buffer, any surprise expense, from a medical bill to a job loss, can push you into high-interest borrowing or force you to sell investments at a bad time.

**Instead:** build a fund covering at least a few months of essential expenses, kept somewhere safe and accessible. Our guide on [emergency funds](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save) explains how much to aim for and where to keep it.

## 2. Relying only on employer health insurance

Group health cover from an employer is valuable, but it usually ends when you leave the job, the cover amount may be modest, and it may not include parents. Buying an individual policy later in life can be harder and more expensive, especially once health conditions appear.

**Instead:** consider having your own health policy as well, and understand the terms that affect claims. See [how to compare health insurance policies](/blog/how-to-compare-health-insurance-policies).

## 3. Mixing insurance with investment

Many people buy endowment or money-back policies expecting both protection and returns. These policies often provide a relatively small life cover for the premium paid, and returns that are hard to compare with other options because charges and bonuses are not always transparent.

**Instead:** ask two separate questions. How much life cover would my dependants need if I died? And where should I invest for my goals? Pure term insurance is designed to answer the first question. Compare the **sum assured** you get against the premium for any policy you consider.

## 4. Paying only the minimum due on a credit card

The "minimum amount due" on a credit card statement is not what you owe; it is the smallest payment that avoids a late-payment charge. The rest of the balance attracts interest, which on credit cards is usually far higher than on other loans. Carrying a balance can also mean new purchases attract interest from the day you make them.

**Instead:** pay the **total amount due** every month. If you cannot, treat the balance as an urgent debt to clear, and stop adding new spending to the card until it is paid off. See [understanding loan interest rates](/blog/understanding-loan-interest-rates).

## 5. Letting EMIs creep up

Phones, appliances, holidays and gadgets are increasingly sold on EMI or "buy now, pay later" terms. Each instalment looks small, but together they can take a large share of your income and leave little room for savings or emergencies. "No-cost EMI" offers can still involve processing fees or a lost cash discount.

**Instead:** add up all your EMIs before taking another, and check whether you could still afford them if your income fell.

## 6. Not knowing where the money goes

Digital payments make spending effortless and easy to underestimate. Many people are surprised when they first total a month of UPI payments, food delivery and subscriptions.

**Instead:** track one month of spending and build a simple plan. Our guide to [creating a monthly budget](/blog/how-to-create-a-monthly-budget-in-india) walks through the steps.

## 7. Keeping all long-term savings in a savings account

Savings accounts are ideal for money you may need soon. For goals ten or twenty years away, money kept only in a savings account may lose purchasing power once inflation and tax are considered.

**Instead:** match each goal to a time horizon, and learn how different options balance risk and return. See [how inflation affects your savings](/blog/how-inflation-affects-your-savings).

## 8. Acting on tips and "guaranteed" returns

Stock tips on messaging apps, social media "gurus", and schemes promising fixed high monthly returns are a common route to losses and fraud. Genuine market investments cannot promise fixed returns, and schemes that do are a major warning sign.

**Instead:**

- Check whether anyone giving investment advice is **registered with SEBI** as an investment adviser or research analyst, using the SEBI website.
- Check whether an entity accepting deposits is authorised to do so, using RBI's [Sachet](https://sachet.rbi.org.in) portal, where you can also report suspicious schemes.
- Never transfer money to personal accounts for "trading", and never install apps or share screens at a stranger's request.

## 9. Not updating nominations

If a bank account, demat account, mutual fund folio or insurance policy has no nominee, or an outdated one, your family may face a long and stressful process to claim the money.

**Instead:** check nominations on every account and policy, and update them after major life events such as marriage or the birth of a child. Nominations are usually quick to update online.

## 10. Signing without reading the terms

Loan agreements, insurance policies and credit card terms contain details that matter: fees, exclusions, waiting periods, rate resets and penalties. Many problems arise because a borrower or policyholder did not know a condition existed.

**Instead:** for loans, read the **Key Fact Statement**. For insurance, read the **policy wording** and the **customer information sheet**. Ask questions in writing before you sign, and keep copies.

## 11. Guaranteeing loans casually

Standing as a guarantor or co-borrower for a friend or relative can feel like a formality. It is not. If the borrower does not repay, the lender can recover the money from you, and missed payments can appear on your credit report.

**Instead:** only guarantee a loan you would be able and willing to repay yourself. See [how credit scores work in India](/blog/how-credit-scores-work-in-india).

## 12. Keeping your family in the dark

Many families do not know what accounts, investments, insurance policies or loans a member has. In an emergency, this can delay access to money when it is needed most.

**Instead:** keep a simple, secure record of your accounts, policies, loans and where documents are kept, and tell a trusted family member where it is. Do not include passwords, PINs or OTPs.

## A note on last-minute tax decisions

A related habit is buying insurance or investment products in a rush near the end of the financial year, mainly to claim a tax deduction. A product bought only for tax reasons may not suit your needs, and under the new tax regime, which is now the default, many of these deductions are not available at all.

**Instead:** decide on your tax regime early in the year and plan investments around your goals first. See [basics of income tax in India](/blog/basics-of-income-tax-in-india).

We think you do not need to fix everything at once. Pick the one mistake on this list that costs you the most today, fix that, and then move to the next. Small, steady improvements tend to last.
