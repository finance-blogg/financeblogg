Health insurance does not always pay 100% of a hospital bill. Many policies use **cost-sharing** terms—commonly discussed as **deductible**, **co-pay** (co-payment) and **co-insurance**. If you misunderstand them, a claim that is “approved” can still leave a large amount for you to pay.

Always treat your **policy wording** as the source of truth. This explainer is educational, not a recommendation of any insurer or plan.

<KeyTakeaways>
- A **deductible** is an amount you bear first (as defined in the policy) before the insurer’s share applies for that cover structure.
- A **co-pay** usually means you pay a **percentage** of the admissible claim; the insurer pays the rest, subject to limits.
- **Co-insurance** is also a sharing ratio; wordings differ—read the definition in *your* policy.
- Room rent limits, sub-limits and [waiting periods](/blog/health-insurance-waiting-periods-and-exclusions) can matter as much as co-pay.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Deductible

In many retail health products, a deductible means that for a claim (or for a year, depending on design), you pay a fixed first slice yourself. Only after that slice—if the wording works that way—does the insurer’s indemnity apply to the remaining admissible amount.

Deductibles can lower premiums. They also raise your out-of-pocket risk for smaller hospitalisations. Whether that trade-off suits you is a personal decision, not something a blog can decide.

## Co-pay (co-payment)

A co-pay clause typically requires you to pay a stated **percentage** of the claim amount that the insurer otherwise finds admissible. Example language in brochures may say the insured bears 10% or 20%—but only the policy schedule and wording control your contract.

Co-pays sometimes apply only for treatment in certain cities, for senior citizens, or for specific treatments. Do not assume a co-pay applies to every line item.

<Callout type="example" title="Illustrative arithmetic">
Suppose an admissible claim is ₹1,00,000 and a co-pay of 20% applies with no other reductions. You would pay ₹20,000 and the insurer ₹80,000—before considering deductibles, sub-limits or non-payable items. Real bills are messier; this is teaching maths only.
</Callout>

## Co-insurance

In insurance language, co-insurance also describes sharing between you and the insurer. Some documents use co-pay and co-insurance in overlapping ways. **Do not rely on a blog synonym list**—search the definitions section of the policy PDF.

## How these interact with other limits

Cost-sharing is only one layer. Also check:

- Sum insured and restore benefits.
- Room rent capping.
- Disease-wise sub-limits.
- Non-payables list (consumables, etc., as per wording).
- Network vs reimbursement rules.

For a wider checklist, see [how to compare health insurance policies](/blog/how-to-compare-health-insurance-policies) and [waiting periods and exclusions](/blog/health-insurance-waiting-periods-and-exclusions).

## Questions to ask before you buy or renew

1. Where is the deductible / co-pay defined—schedule, base wording, or both?
2. Does co-pay apply to all claims or only some?
3. What happens with cashless network hospitals vs reimbursement?
4. How do sub-limits change the effective share you pay?

<Callout type="warning" title="Sales scripts vs wording">
If a seller’s explanation disagrees with the PDF, trust the PDF—or get written clarification from the insurer before you rely on the promise.
</Callout>
