An **EMI** is a fixed rupee amount you pay each month so that a loan of a given size, rate and tenure is fully repaid by the last instalment. The amount is “equated” (the same each month, unless the rate or tenure is reset). The **split** between interest and principal inside that amount is not equal, and it is not supposed to be.

This article explains the usual reducing-balance EMI used for home, car and personal loans in India. It does not tell you whether to borrow.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Most retail EMIs use **reducing balance**: interest is charged on the principal still outstanding, not on the original amount for the whole tenure.
- Early EMIs are **mostly interest**. Later EMIs are **mostly principal**.
- Stretching tenure **lowers the EMI** and **raises total interest**.
- A floating rate can change the EMI, the tenure, or both, when the benchmark moves.
- Compare loans on **APR-style cost**, fees and prepayment rules, not on EMI alone. See [loan interest rates](/blog/understanding-loan-interest-rates).
</KeyTakeaways>

## The three numbers that set an EMI

For a reducing-balance loan you need:

1. **Principal** — the amount disbursed (not the property price).
2. **Interest rate** — usually an annual rate, applied monthly as rate ÷ 12.
3. **Tenure** — number of months.

Lenders publish an EMI using a standard formula. Many bank websites have a calculator. Treat the **sanction letter and repayment schedule** as the contract, not a third-party app that skipped fees.

<Callout type="example" title="Same principal, two tenures">
Illustrative only. ₹5 lakh at a flat 12% a year reducing balance (1% a month).

- 36 months: the EMI is higher; you finish sooner; total interest is lower.
- 60 months: the EMI is lower; you pay interest for two extra years; total interest is higher.

The “cheap EMI” is often the expensive loan. Work the **total of EMIs minus principal**, not only the monthly debit.
</Callout>

## Why the first year feels like you owe the same

Each month the lender:

1. computes interest on the **current** outstanding;
2. takes that interest out of your EMI;
3. uses whatever remains to cut principal.

At the start, outstanding is large, so interest eats most of the EMI. Principal barely moves. This is amortisation, not a trick unique to your bank.

That is why a prepayment in the early years (where the product allows it) cuts more future interest than the same rupee paid later. Read [prepayment and foreclosure](/blog/how-loan-prepayment-and-foreclosure-work) before you assume you can do this free.

## Flat rate vs reducing rate

Some ads still quote a **flat** rate: interest on the original principal for the whole tenure, then divided into months. The rupee EMI can look similar to a reducing-balance loan whose **reducing** rate is much higher.

Always ask: is the schedule reducing balance? What is the **annual effective** cost including processing fee? RBI has required clearer communication of rates; still, the schedule is the document that matters.

## Floating rates and EMI resets

On a floating home loan, the rate is tied to a benchmark (for many retail loans, an external benchmark such as the repo-linked rate). When the benchmark changes:

- the bank may **raise or cut the EMI**, or
- keep the EMI and **lengthen or shorten tenure**, or
- use a mix.

Your first EMI is not a promise for 20 years. When you read an [RBI policy decision](/blog/how-to-read-an-rbi-monetary-policy-statement), transmission to your loan is neither instant nor one-for-one.

## Fees that sit outside the EMI

The EMI does not usually include:

- processing or login fees deducted from disbursal;
- stamp duty, legal and technical charges on a home loan;
- compulsory insurance if the lender bundled it;
- penalty interest on a bounced NACH.

If ₹10 lakh is sanctioned and ₹8,000 is taken as fees from disbursal, you still owe interest as the contract states — often on the amount the schedule shows, which you must read.

## How to use a repayment schedule

Ask for an amortisation table. For each of the first 12 months, note interest vs principal. Check:

- whether GST applies on any fee (not on interest itself in the usual bank loan);
- the due date versus salary date, so the NACH does not bounce;
- part-prepayment: does it cut EMI, cut tenure, or wait for an instruction?

Missed EMIs damage the [credit score](/blog/how-credit-scores-work-in-india) and add penal charges. The formula assumes you pay on time.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think the useful question is not “what EMI can the calculator show as affordable?” but “what EMI still leaves room for an [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save) after this debit?” An EMI that fits only on paper is how households become one medical bill away from default.
</Callout>

## Checklist before you sign

1. Principal, rate type (fixed or floating), benchmark, spread, tenure.
2. Sample schedule for the first year.
3. Total interest if the rate never changes (a baseline, not a forecast).
4. What happens to EMI and tenure if the rate rises by 1 percentage point — ask the lender to show it.
5. Prepayment rules and bounce charges, in the agreement.

If the EMI only works by stretching tenure into retirement, the problem is the loan size, not the calculator.
