Income tax can seem complicated, but the core logic is straightforward: add up your income, subtract what the law allows, apply the tax rates, and compare the result with tax already paid. This guide explains each step in plain language for individual taxpayers.

<Callout type="verify" title="Tax rules change every year">
Income tax slabs, rebates and deductions are set by the annual Finance Act. In addition, the **Income-tax Act, 2025** replaced the Income-tax Act, 1961 from **1 April 2026**, with new section numbers and some new terms, such as "tax year". The figures in this article are taken from **Union Budget 2025 for financial year 2025-26** to illustrate the method. Always check the rules for the year you are filing on the official [Income Tax Department](https://www.incometax.gov.in) website.
</Callout>

<KeyTakeaways>
- Income is grouped under five "heads", such as salary and capital gains.
- Individuals choose between the **new tax regime** (the default, with lower rates and few deductions) and the **old tax regime** (higher rates, more deductions).
- Tax is calculated slab by slab: only the income **within** each slab is taxed at that slab's rate.
- **TDS** and **advance tax** are prepayments. Your return settles the final amount.
- Check your **Form 26AS** and **Annual Information Statement** before filing.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Who has to pay income tax

Individuals pay tax on their income above the basic exemption limit. How your income is taxed depends on your **residential status**, which is based mainly on how many days you spent in India. Broadly, residents are taxed on their worldwide income, while non-residents are taxed only on income earned or received in India. The detailed rules have conditions and exceptions, so check them if you live or work abroad for part of the year.

Even if your income is below the taxable limit, you may still need to, or benefit from, filing a return, for example to claim a refund of TDS.

## The five heads of income

| Head | Examples |
| --- | --- |
| Salaries | Salary, allowances, bonuses, perquisites, pension |
| Income from house property | Rent from a property you own |
| Profits and gains of business or profession | Income from a business, freelancing or professional practice |
| Capital gains | Profit from selling shares, mutual fund units, property or gold |
| Income from other sources | Interest from savings accounts and deposits, dividends, certain gifts |

Interest income is a frequently forgotten item. Interest from savings accounts and fixed deposits is taxable and must be reported, even if the bank has already deducted TDS. See [savings account vs fixed deposit](/blog/savings-account-vs-fixed-deposit).

## Old regime vs new regime

Since financial year 2023-24, the **new tax regime** has been the default for individuals. You can opt for the old regime instead if it suits you.

| | New regime | Old regime |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tax rates | Lower, with more slabs | Higher |
| Deductions and exemptions | Very few. A standard deduction is available to salaried individuals and pensioners | Many, such as investments and payments under the old section 80C, health insurance premiums, house rent allowance and home loan interest |
| Suits | People with few deductions to claim | People with large eligible deductions |

Salaried individuals without business income can generally choose the regime each year when filing their return. People with business or professional income face restrictions on switching back and forth, so check the rules.

## How tax is calculated, step by step

1. **Add up gross total income** from all five heads.
2. **Subtract allowable deductions** (very few under the new regime).
3. **Apply the slab rates** to your taxable income. Some income, such as certain capital gains, is taxed at special rates instead of slab rates.
4. **Subtract any rebate** you are eligible for.
5. **Add health and education cess**, currently 4% of the tax.
6. **Subtract tax already paid** through TDS, TCS and advance tax. The result is tax payable or a refund due.

### Slabs are applied in layers

A common misconception is that crossing into a higher slab means **all** your income is taxed at the higher rate. It does not. Only the portion of income within each slab is taxed at that slab's rate.

### New regime slabs for FY 2025-26 (illustration)

| Taxable income | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Up to ₹4,00,000 | Nil |
| ₹4,00,001 to ₹8,00,000 | 5% |
| ₹8,00,001 to ₹12,00,000 | 10% |
| ₹12,00,001 to ₹16,00,000 | 15% |
| ₹16,00,001 to ₹20,00,000 | 20% |
| ₹20,00,001 to ₹24,00,000 | 25% |
| Above ₹24,00,000 | 30% |

For FY 2025-26, salaried individuals could claim a standard deduction of ₹75,000 under the new regime, and a rebate meant that residents with taxable income up to ₹12,00,000 paid no tax under the new regime, apart from tax on special-rate income such as certain capital gains.

### Old regime slabs for FY 2025-26 (illustration, individuals below 60)

| Taxable income | Rate |
| --- | --- |
| Up to ₹2,50,000 | Nil |
| ₹2,50,001 to ₹5,00,000 | 5% |
| ₹5,00,001 to ₹10,00,000 | 20% |
| Above ₹10,00,000 | 30% |

Higher exemption limits apply to senior and very senior citizens under the old regime, and a smaller rebate applies to taxable income up to ₹5,00,000. Surcharge applies at higher income levels under both regimes.

### Worked examples

<Callout type="example" title="Salaried, new regime, FY 2025-26 rules">
These examples use FY 2025-26 rules for illustration only. They ignore surcharge and special-rate income.

**Example 1: gross salary ₹14,00,000**

- Less standard deduction: ₹75,000
- Taxable income: ₹13,25,000
- Tax on first ₹4,00,000: nil
- ₹4,00,001 to ₹8,00,000 at 5%: ₹20,000
- ₹8,00,001 to ₹12,00,000 at 10%: ₹40,000
- ₹12,00,001 to ₹13,25,000 at 15%: ₹18,750
- Tax before cess: ₹78,750
- Add 4% cess: ₹3,150
- **Total tax: ₹81,900**

**Example 2: gross salary ₹11,75,000**

- Less standard deduction: ₹75,000
- Taxable income: ₹11,00,000
- Tax by slabs: ₹20,000 + ₹30,000 = ₹50,000
- Rebate: the full ₹50,000, because taxable income is within the rebate limit
- **Total tax: nil**
</Callout>

To compare regimes for your own situation, the e-filing portal provides a tax calculator. Enter the same figures under both regimes and compare the result.

## TDS, Form 26AS and the AIS

**Tax deducted at source (TDS)** is tax collected before income reaches you. Employers deduct TDS from salary based on your expected annual tax and issue **Form 16**. Banks deduct TDS on interest above a threshold, and other payers deduct it on various payments.

Two statements on the e-filing portal show what has been reported against your PAN:

- **Form 26AS** shows tax deducted or collected and tax paid.
- **The Annual Information Statement (AIS)** shows a wider range of reported information, such as interest, dividends, securities transactions and some high-value purchases. A simplified **Taxpayer Information Summary (TIS)** is derived from it.

Check both before filing. If something is wrong, you can submit feedback on the AIS through the portal.

## Advance tax

If your tax liability for the year, after TDS, is **₹10,000 or more**, you are generally expected to pay **advance tax** in instalments during the year rather than all at the end. For most individuals, the due dates have been 15 June, 15 September, 15 December and 15 March, with cumulative instalments of 15%, 45%, 75% and 100% of the estimated tax. Resident senior citizens without business income are exempt. Late or short payment attracts interest.

This mostly affects people with significant income that has no TDS or too little TDS, such as freelancers, business owners, landlords, or investors with large capital gains.

## Filing your income tax return (ITR)

You file your return online on the e-filing portal. The general steps:

1. Collect Form 16, interest certificates, capital gains statements and other documents.
2. Check Form 26AS and the AIS.
3. Choose the correct ITR form. For example, ITR-1 is for resident individuals with simpler income such as salary, one house property and interest, within an income limit; other forms cover capital gains, business income and more complex cases.
4. Choose your tax regime, enter or verify the pre-filled data and calculate tax.
5. Pay any balance tax, then submit the return.
6. **E-verify** the return, for example using an Aadhaar OTP or net banking. A return that is not verified within the allowed time is treated as not filed.

For individuals whose accounts do not need to be audited, the due date has generally been **31 July** after the end of the financial year, though the government sometimes extends it. Filing late can lead to a fee, interest and the loss of some benefits, such as carrying forward certain losses.

## A short primer on GST

**Goods and Services Tax (GST)** is an indirect tax on the supply of goods and services. Unlike income tax, most individuals do not file GST returns. You pay GST as part of the price of what you buy, and the seller collects it and pays it to the government.

GST matters to individuals in a few situations:

- **Freelancers and small businesses** must register for GST once their turnover crosses the applicable threshold, which differs for goods and services and for some states. Some activities require registration regardless of turnover.
- **Invoices** for business purchases show GST separately, which matters if you can claim input tax credit.
- **Rates** vary by item. The GST Council revised the rate structure in 2025, simplifying it into fewer main slabs.

<Callout type="verify">
GST thresholds and rates are set by the GST Council and change through notifications. Check the [CBIC GST](https://cbic-gst.gov.in) and [GST portal](https://www.gst.gov.in) websites for the current position, and consult a GST practitioner if you run a business.
</Callout>

## Common mistakes to avoid

- **Not reporting interest income**, assuming TDS settles it.
- **Not checking the AIS**, then receiving a notice for a mismatch.
- **Picking a regime without comparing** the two.
- **Missing the due date or forgetting to e-verify.**
- **Buying products in March only for a deduction** you may not even be able to claim under the new regime. See [common personal finance mistakes](/blog/common-personal-finance-mistakes).
