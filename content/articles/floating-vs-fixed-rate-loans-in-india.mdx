When you borrow, the **interest rate type** decides how your cost can change after disbursement. In India, retail loans are commonly offered as **floating** (benchmark-linked) or **fixed** for a period—or as hybrids. Understanding the label on your sanction letter matters more than a viral “always choose X” tip.

This article explains the mechanics. It does not recommend a loan product or bank.

<KeyTakeaways>
- **Floating** rates can move when the lender’s benchmark and spread rules reset; EMI or tenure may change.
- **Fixed** rates stay fixed only as defined in the contract—check the fixed period and what happens after.
- Compare **APR-style costs, fees and reset clauses**, not only the teaser percentage on a banner.
- Prepayment rules often differ by rate type; read [prepayment and foreclosure](/blog/how-loan-prepayment-and-foreclosure-work) alongside this.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Floating rate, in one paragraph

A floating-rate loan is linked to an external or internal **benchmark** plus a **spread** (margin) the lender sets for your profile. When the benchmark changes under the loan’s reset rules, your applicable rate can change. Lenders may adjust **EMI**, **tenure**, or a mix—your agreement and account statements show which method applies.

Repo-linked and other external-benchmark linked home loans are widely discussed in RBI communications; always read *your* sanction terms rather than assuming every floating loan behaves identically.

## Fixed rate, in one paragraph

A fixed-rate loan keeps the contractual rate unchanged for the **fixed period** stated in the documents. That can mean predictability for budgeting. It does not automatically mean the cheapest lifetime cost, and some “fixed” offers later convert to floating or reprice after the fixed window. The brochure headline is incomplete without the conversion clause.

## What can change on a floating loan

After a rate reset, typical outcomes include:

- **Higher rate → higher EMI**, tenure unchanged; or  
- **Higher rate → longer tenure**, EMI held; or  
- a combination within policy limits.

The reverse can happen when rates fall, subject to the same contract. Ask the lender which lever they use by default and whether you can request a change.

<Callout type="example" title="Illustrative only">
If EMI stays constant while the rate rises, more of each instalment may go to interest and less to principal early on, so the loan can last longer. The exact path depends on your outstanding principal and reset maths—use the lender’s revised schedule, not a social-media calculator alone.
</Callout>

## Documents to read before you choose

1. **Sanction letter / key fact statement:** rate type, benchmark name, spread, reset frequency.  
2. **Fees:** processing, conversion (fixed↔floating), foreclosure.  
3. **EMI schedule logic** after reset.  
4. **Prepayment** permissions and charges for your rate type.  
5. Whether a **teaser** or introductory rate applies for the first months only.

For how an EMI is built from principal, rate and tenure, see [how loan EMI is calculated](/blog/how-loan-emi-is-calculated). For vocabulary such as reducing balance, see [understanding loan interest rates](/blog/understanding-loan-interest-rates).

## Budgeting differences

- **Floating:** plan a buffer for EMI or tenure shocks; your [quarterly money review](/blog/how-to-do-a-quarterly-money-review-in-india) should re-check loan statements after policy cycles.  
- **Fixed (for the fixed term):** EMI planning is steadier, but refinancing or conversion fees may apply later if you want to switch.

Neither structure removes the need for an [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save). A rate cut does not replace cash buffers; a rate hike should not be the first time you discover thin savings.

## Questions worth asking the lender

1. What exact **benchmark** and **spread** apply to me?  
2. How often does the rate **reset**, and will I get an updated schedule?  
3. On reset, do you change **EMI**, **tenure**, or both?  
4. What are **conversion** and **foreclosure** charges for this rate type?  
5. Is any advertised rate a **limited-period** rate?

Write the answers down. Screenshots of ads are not the contract.

<Callout type="verify">
RBI directions on retail lending, external benchmarks and foreclosure charges have evolved over time and can differ by loan category. Confirm the latest rules and your loan agreement before relying on a remembered “all floating home loans have zero foreclosure fee” claim.
</Callout>

## Bottom line

Floating vs fixed is a **risk-sharing** choice: who bears interest-rate movement during the loan life, and under what clauses. Read the reset and conversion language, then map EMI risk to your income stability—not to a one-line tip on the internet.
