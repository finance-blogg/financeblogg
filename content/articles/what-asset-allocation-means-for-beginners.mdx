**Asset allocation** is the decision of how much of your investable money sits in **different types** of assets — cash and deposits, bonds or debt funds, shares or equity funds, and sometimes gold or property — given when you need the money and how much of a drop you can live with.

It is not a stock tip, and it is not the same as buying five schemes that all own the same large-cap names. This article is education. It does not give you a pie chart to copy.

<KeyTakeaways>
- Allocation is about **asset classes**, not about the number of folios.
- **Time horizon** and **the job of the money** should drive the mix more than last year’s winner.
- Cash for near-term bills is an allocation choice, not a failure to “stay invested”.
- Rebalancing means returning to your mix when markets move it, not chasing the hot sleeve.
- [Diversification](/blog/what-diversification-means-for-beginner-investors) is how you spread risk **inside** a class; allocation is how much you give each class.
</KeyTakeaways>

## Asset classes, in household language

For a beginner in India, the useful buckets are:

- **Money you can spend soon:** savings account, overnight or liquid-style products, very short deposits. Purchasing power leaks through [inflation](/blog/how-inflation-affects-your-savings), but the rupee amount is relatively stable.
- **Money you lend:** FDs, PPF, government small savings, debt mutual funds. You are exposed to interest-rate moves and, in some products, to credit risk. You are not owning businesses.
- **Money you own in businesses:** shares, equity mutual funds, some hybrid funds. The value can fall for years. The historical reason people accept that is long-run growth, which is **not guaranteed**.
- **Other:** gold, real estate, commodities. Each has its own costs, taxes and liquidity. Owning a house you live in is shelter first, an “asset class” second.

If you cannot explain which bucket a product sits in, you are not allocating. You are collecting.

## Allocation is not diversification

Buying four flexi-cap funds is still mostly **equity**. That is a diversification attempt inside one class, and it may not even diversify if the portfolios overlap.

A simple example of allocation: ₹10 lakh earmarked for a house down payment in 18 months should not sit 80% in small-cap funds, however diversified those funds are. A simple example of diversification: if you do own equity for a 20-year goal, one country, one sector, or one employer’s ESOP is a concentration, not a plan.

<Callout type="example" title="Same products, different jobs">
Illustrative only. Two people each invest ₹5,000 a month.

- A has rent due in 11 months for a security deposit top-up. Most of that SIP-sized surplus belongs in deposits or cash, not in equity, because the date is close.
- B is 30, funding retirement around age 60, with an [emergency fund](/blog/emergency-fund-how-much-should-you-save) already separate. A larger equity share can be *considered* because the horizon is long — which still is not a recommendation of any scheme.
</Callout>

## What actually sets the mix

**Horizon.** Money needed inside two years generally cannot afford an equity winter. Money needed after a decade can, **if** you will not sell in a panic.

**Other income and obligations.** A household with a single income and a large EMI already has a “short bond” called the loan. Adding a high equity share on the side is not the same risk as it is for a dual-income household with no debt.

**Behaviour.** An allocation you will abandon after a 20% fall is the wrong allocation, even if a formula said it was efficient.

Age-based rules of thumb (“100 minus age in equity”) are slogans. They ignore the house, the employer stock, the [PPF](/blog/how-public-provident-fund-works-in-india) you already hold, and the date of the child’s college fees.

## Rebalancing without drama

If equity has a good year, it becomes a larger share of the pie even if you did nothing. Rebalancing is selling some of what grew (or directing new SIPs to what shrank) to restore the weights you chose.

Do it on a calendar (once a year) or when weights drift past a band you wrote down. Do not rebalance because a television panel is excited. Costs, taxes and exit loads apply; see how [mutual funds](/blog/understanding-sip-and-mutual-fund-investing) actually charge you.

## Mistakes that look like allocation

- Putting the emergency fund into a credit-risk debt fund because the chart looked “like an FD”.
- Treating a 3-year lock-in equity product as safe because you cannot sell it.
- Copying a 25-year-old creator’s pie chart when you have a wedding in 14 months.
- Counting the house and the NPS and three ELSS funds as four independent ideas without adding the equity total.

<Callout type="opinion">
We think beginners should write the mix in rupees next to each [goal](/blog/how-to-set-financial-goals-in-india), not as a lifestyle brand. “60/40” means nothing until you know 60% of *what*, and *when* that money must work.
</Callout>

## A practical sequence

1. Separate cash for emergencies and near bills.
2. List goals and dates.
3. Assign each goal a dominant bucket (spend-soon / lend / own).
4. Only then choose products *inside* the bucket — FD vs PPF vs debt fund, or index vs active equity — using costs and understanding, not ads.
5. Write when you will review. Life events beat market events.
